# Muse Ember Is Not Three Times Cheaper Than Haiku. Read the Fine Print.

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 10, 2026

Meta launched a new model on November 9 called Muse Ember. The headlines wrote themselves: "matches Haiku 5.5 at a third of the cost." Social media shortened it further: "Ember is 3x cheaper than Haiku."

It's a good headline. It's also misleading. And the proof is on Meta's own announcement page, which I read myself.

Here's what Meta actually wrote: "Muse Ember delivers more intelligence per dollar than Claude Haiku 5.5 or GPT-6 Luna at any reasoning setting: it matches Haiku's best score at about a third of the cost and beats every Luna setting."

A third of the cost of what? The answer is on the same page, in the price list: "It costs $0.10 per million input tokens, $0.004 per million cached input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens."

Ten cents in. Fifty cents out. That is the exact same list price as Claude Haiku 5.5. Same price per token, to the penny.

So where does "a third of the cost" come from? From how many tokens each model burns to finish a job. On the Artificial Analysis benchmark, Ember scored 36 — the same as Haiku — but it used 115 million output tokens for the whole test where Haiku used 298 million. Less than half the tokens, same score. That works out to about $0.14 per task for Ember versus about $0.41 for Haiku. A third of the cost per task. Not per token. Per task.

That's a real advantage. Using fewer tokens to get the same answer is genuinely good engineering, and for high-volume agent work it adds up to real money. But it is not a price cut, and anyone who buys Ember thinking the tokens cost a third as much is going to be surprised by the bill.

There's one more piece of fine print, also on Meta's own page. Ember's price triples above 200,000 input tokens in a request — $0.30 in, $1.50 out. Haiku's price jumps fivefold above 100,000 tokens. So which model is actually cheaper for you depends on how long your prompts are. Short prompts, Ember wins on efficiency. Long prompts, the math changes, and you have to run your own numbers.

So what does this mean for a working person? It means when any company says "a third of the cost," you ask three questions: a third of what — per token, or per task? At what prompt length? And what's the price when you go past the limit? Those three answers change everything. The headline gives you one number. The price list gives you the truth.

Meta's model is good. The headline just isn't honest about why.

*Source: Meta AI Research, "Introducing Our Smallest, Most Efficient Model: Muse Ember," November 9, 2026 — https://research.meta.ai/blog/muse-ember (read Nov 10, 2026). Core debunk first aired in Claims Checked Ep. 22.*
