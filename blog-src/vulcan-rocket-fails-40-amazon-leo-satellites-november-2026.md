# A Rocket Failed Sunday — and 40 Amazon Satellites Went With It

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 16, 2026

On Sunday afternoon, a rocket went up from Cape Canaveral carrying 40 satellites for Amazon's new internet network. Ten minutes later, it was over. The rocket failed, the satellites never reached orbit, and every one of them was lost.

The rocket was United Launch Alliance's Vulcan. It lifted off at 12:40 p.m. Eastern on Sunday, November 15, from Space Launch Complex 41 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. ULA confirmed about 50 minutes after liftoff that the vehicle "suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit." As of Monday morning, ULA has not said what part of the rocket failed.

This one stings because of the track record behind it. It was the first complete mission failure in ULA's roughly 20-year history — the first outright failure since the company was formed in 2006. ULA is a joint venture between Boeing and Lockheed Martin, and it has built its whole reputation on reliability. That reputation took a real hit on Sunday.

It was also supposed to be a comeback flight. This was Vulcan's first launch in nine months. The last Vulcan flight, a national security mission on February 12, was marred by a solid rocket booster malfunction, and the rocket had been grounded ever since. The boosters on Sunday's flight appeared to work normally — they burned out about a minute and 40 seconds in, right on plan. Something else went wrong later in the flight, and engineers now have a new fault to find.

The payload was 40 satellites for Amazon Leo, the company's satellite internet service — the one meant to compete with Starlink. Before Sunday, Amazon had about 400 Leo satellites already in orbit, launched on 15 missions using three other rockets. The plan calls for 3,236 satellites in total, so the network was only about 12 percent built before this flight. The 40 lost satellites would have brought the total to roughly 440. The payload weighed more than 50,000 pounds, a company record for ULA and the largest batch of satellites Amazon had ever put on one launch.

What happens next is a process, not a guess. The Federal Aviation Administration has said ULA must conduct a mishap investigation, and ULA will have to submit a final report for approval that determines the root cause and identifies corrective actions. No timeline has been set. ULA said it holds itself "to the standard of mission success" and that on Sunday it "did not meet that standard."

The failure ripples beyond Amazon. Sunday's mission was meant to be the kickoff of a 38-launch contract between ULA and Amazon. Amazon is not stuck, though — its next mission is already scheduled, using an Ariane 6 rocket to carry 36 more satellites, no earlier than November 23 from French Guiana. And Delta Air Lines, which has committed to installing Amazon Leo Wi-Fi on an initial 500 aircraft beginning in 2028, will be watching how fast the schedule recovers.

Here's the part that matters to folks who work with their hands. Rockets fail. Cranes tip. Concrete cures wrong. A perfect record doesn't mean the next job goes right — it means the system that built the record has to be honest about what broke, find the cause, and fix it before the next one. ULA has 20 years of doing exactly that. Sunday tested whether that still holds. The investigation will tell us.
