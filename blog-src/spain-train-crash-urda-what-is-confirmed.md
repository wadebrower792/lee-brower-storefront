# Spain's Train Crash at Urda: What Is Confirmed, and What Is Not Yet

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, October 30, 2026

On Wednesday, October 28, 2026, two high-speed trains collided near Urda, in Toledo province in central Spain, on the Madrid–Seville route. It is Spain's deadliest train accident since 2013, and it is also a story where early numbers moved fast and some of them were wrong. Here is what is confirmed as of October 30, and what investigators have explicitly not settled yet.

## What happened

A Renfe high-speed train ran into the back of a stationary Iryo train near Urda. The Renfe train carried 379 passengers and seven crew; the Iryo train carried 147 passengers and five crew, according to reporting of the preliminary investigation. The collision derailed carriages and caused mass casualties. The death toll stood at 68 on Wednesday and was raised to at least 69 on Thursday, October 29, by Castilla-La Mancha regional president Emiliano García-Page after one of the injured died in hospital. That figure of at least 69 is the official toll as of October 30.

Two cautions belong with that number. First, the Civil Guard said it has received reports of 64 people missing, and it is unclear how many of the missing are among bodies still to be identified — so the toll and the missing count cannot simply be added together. Second, an investigation-body case file briefly showed 74 dead on Thursday before being corrected back to 69, which Spain's Transport Ministry did not confirm. If you see the 74 figure still circulating, it was a corrected error, not a later update.

## What the preliminary report says

Spain's Independent Authority for the Technical Investigation of Accidents and Incidents, known as AITAT, issued a preliminary report that estimates the Renfe train was travelling between 160 and 200 kph (about 99 to 124 mph) when it hit the stationary train. That is a preliminary estimate, not a confirmed speed: AITAT said it cannot confirm the exact speed until it extracts information from the train's black box.

The sequence the preliminary report describes began with a signalling failure. Several trains were automatically stopped after the automatic signalling system failed, and the central control centre in Madrid took over. According to Transport Minister Óscar Puente, the Renfe train was then authorised to proceed even though the Iryo train was stationary ahead of it. Audio published by El País showed a controller authorising the Renfe train to continue at normal speed, which Puente said disregarded a protocol to travel at no more than 40 kph in such circumstances. The audio also showed the Renfe train was authorised to resume before the Iryo train in front, when protocol called for the Iryo train to proceed first.

AITAT summed up the preliminary finding this way: a sequence of malfunctions, instructions, and actions led to the collision, in the absence of the necessary mitigating barriers. That is a description of a chain, not a verdict on any one person. The judicial and technical investigations remain active, and no individual has been found legally responsible. A fatal accident in January 2026 involved the same companies on the same line.

## What to watch next

Three things will settle the open questions: the black-box data, which fixes the actual speed; the full technical report, which tests whether the signalling failure alone explains the chain or whether fallback procedures failed too; and the identification process, which is proceeding slowly as families are contacted. Until those land, any account that names a single cause or a final death toll is getting ahead of the investigators.

For the rest of us, the lesson is a familiar one from any jobsite: when a safety system fails, the fallback procedure is the real safety system. A protocol that says 40 kph in degraded mode only works if the person giving the order follows it — and the chain of decisions after a failure deserves the same scrutiny as the failure itself.
