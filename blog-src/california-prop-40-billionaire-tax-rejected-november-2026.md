# California Voters Said No to the Billionaire Tax

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 10, 2026

California voters rejected Proposition 40, the one-time 5 percent tax on billionaires, and the Associated Press called the race on Monday, November 9. With about 83 percent of ballots counted, the "no" side led 52.8 percent to 47.2 percent — a margin of roughly 621,000 votes. The Secretary of State certifies the final result on December 11.

The measure would have put a one-time 5 percent levy on the net worth of California residents worth more than $1 billion, applying to anyone who lived in the state on January 1, 2026. Backers estimated it would raise about $100 billion over five years, with most of the money going to Medi-Cal, the state's Medicaid program. About 200 billionaires would have been covered.

It was a strange fight. The measure was backed by SEIU United Healthcare Workers West, a healthcare workers' union, and designed by economists Gabriel Zucman and Emmanuel Saez. But it was opposed by California's own Democratic governor, Gavin Newsom, and by the state's Democratic establishment. The California Teachers Association and Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California also opposed it, arguing it could hurt the state's finances by driving wealthy taxpayers away.

## The money behind the "no"

The opposition outspent supporters by roughly ten to one. Google co-founder Sergey Brin alone put more than $100 million into the fight, and the main opposition committee raised more than $333 million in total. The "yes" side raised about $33.5 million. Brin also backed a competing measure, Proposition 42, which would ban new taxes on personal property — a measure aimed at blocking wealth taxes like this one. As of Monday evening, Proposition 42 was leading 51.6 percent to 48.3 percent, though the AP had not yet called it.

The polling told the story before the votes did. An October survey by UC Berkeley's Institute of Governmental Studies found 50 percent of likely voters opposed the tax and 42 percent in favor. A Public Policy Institute of California poll the same month found 52 percent opposed and 46 percent in favor. Support had been sliding for weeks before Election Day.

## What it means

Supporters had hoped California would start a national debate about taxing billionaire wealth. Instead, the bluest state in the country said no — even in a midterm where Democrats won big almost everywhere else. That is the headline, and it is worth sitting with: voters who just handed Democrats sweeping wins still turned down this tax.

Backers say they could put a similar tax back on the ballot in 2028. But the size of this loss, and the size of the money spent to defeat it, will make that a harder sell next time. A campaign that costs more than $333 million to beat is a campaign the other side will fund again.

For working folks, the lesson is simpler than the politics. A tax on billionaires sounds like it has nothing to do with a carpenter in Alabama — until you remember that the same playbook runs everywhere. A ballot measure, a pile of outside money, and a promise that somebody else will pay for it. California voters did the math and decided the promise did not add up. Whether you agree with them or not, that is what the numbers say: 52.8 to 47.2, called by the AP, with the count still coming in.
