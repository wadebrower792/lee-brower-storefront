# Halloween Night Is the Riskiest Night for Young Pedestrians. The Data Says Why.

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, October 30, 2026

Halloween is tomorrow, and it is worth saying plainly before the costumes come out: Halloween evening is the riskiest night of the year for pedestrians in the United States, and the risk is steepest for small children. That is not a scare line. It is what four decades of federal crash data show.

## What the study found

Researchers examined every fatal U.S. traffic crash from 1975 to 2016 using National Highway Traffic Safety Administration records, comparing pedestrian deaths during Halloween evening hours with deaths during the same hours on control evenings one week before and after. The result, published in JAMA Pediatrics in 2019: the relative risk of a pedestrian fatality was 43 percent higher on Halloween evening, working out to roughly four additional deaths per Halloween night nationwide over the study period. Children aged 4 to 8 faced the steepest increase, with roughly ten times the odds of pedestrian death on Halloween compared with a typical evening, although the absolute risk remained low.

Two limits on that study matter. It is observational, and its data ends in 2016, so it cannot by itself prove how much any single safety measure lowers risk. And the "four additional deaths" figure is a national average across the study period, not a prediction for any one town or any one night. Johns Hopkins Children's Center experts released updated Halloween guidance on October 12, 2026, pointing to the national increase in pedestrian injuries and deaths around the holiday as a central concern for families planning to trick-or-treat.

## What actually helps, according to the guidance

The guidance centers on the basics, and the basics are unglamorous: an adult walking with younger children, reflective gear or a flashlight so drivers can see them, crossing at corners and crosswalks instead of darting between parked cars, and drivers slowing down and watching for children during the peak trick-or-treat hours. Most child pedestrian deaths happen mid-block, not at intersections, which is why the "don't dart into the street" rule matters more than any costume choice. Costumes should let a child see and be seen: face paint instead of a mask that blocks vision where possible, and something reflective on the costume itself.

None of this means skipping trick-or-treating. It means treating the few hours around dusk on October 31 as a jobsite with traffic in it: plan the route, keep the crew together, stay visible, and watch the road before the candy. A safe night is a counted night — same rule as a safe job.
