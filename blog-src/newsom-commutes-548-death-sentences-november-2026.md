# California's Death Row Is Being Emptied: Newsom Commutes All 548 Sentences

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 6, 2026

On Thursday, November 5, California Governor Gavin Newsom announced he is commuting the death sentences of all 548 people on the state's death row to life in prison without the possibility of parole. It is the largest mass commutation of death sentences in American history, according to the Death Penalty Information Center, and it would cut the national death-row population by more than a quarter.

Newsom made the announcement in an open letter to Californians in his final weeks as governor. He wrote that state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California's values, undermines the foundations of the legal system, and diminishes everyone. He called the decision one of the most difficult of his career, and acknowledged the families of victims in the same letter. The 548 people affected include 529 men and 19 women.

## What it does — and what it does not do

The commutations change sentences. They do not repeal the death penalty. California voters kept the death penalty in 2012 and again in 2016, and prosecutors can still seek death sentences in new cases. Newsom cannot repeal the law himself; he is using his constitutional clemency power over the sentences that already exist.

California has not carried out an execution since January 17, 2006 — twenty years ago. Newsom imposed a moratorium on executions shortly after taking office in 2019, so the machinery has been stopped for years. This move takes the next step: it removes the sentences themselves, not just the executions.

Not every commutation is final yet. Under California's Constitution, clemency for anyone convicted of more than one felony requires a recommendation from the California Supreme Court, with four of the seven justices agreeing. About two-thirds of the people on death row fall into that group — 342 of them, according to the San Luis Obispo County District Attorney's Office. The Death Penalty Information Center says the grants take effect immediately for about one-third of the condemned, while the rest wait on court review. Newsom asked the court to issue its recommendation by the end of the year so he can act before his term ends on January 4, when Governor-elect Xavier Becerra takes over.

## The reaction was fast and sharp

Prosecutors and victims' advocates pushed back immediately. Riverside County District Attorney Mike Hestrin said Newsom did not notify the state's district attorneys or get input from victims' families before acting. The San Luis Obispo District Attorney's Office said it has asked the Supreme Court justices to review each case on its own facts and hear from victims' families. Some victim advocates said they will challenge the decision in court.

The other side of the argument is the one Newsom made in his letter: that the death penalty is unjust and error-prone. That is the claim at the center of this fight, and it is not a new one — it is the same argument that has kept California from executing anyone for twenty years.

Whatever the courts decide in the next two months, the practical effect is already clear. The largest death row in the Western Hemisphere is being emptied by a governor in his final weeks, and the question of whether the sentences stay commuted now sits with seven justices in Sacramento. It is a legal fight now, not a political speech — and it will be decided on the constitution, case by case.
