# The Man Who Built the AI Boom Just Resigned

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 12, 2026

Jensen Huang is out at Nvidia. The company announced Thursday morning that Huang, the co-founder who ran Nvidia for 33 years and turned it into the company at the center of the whole artificial-intelligence boom, has resigned as chief executive.

The resignation came after two extramarital affairs became public — one with a woman the Wall Street Journal describes as a suspected Chinese spy, and another with an Nvidia employee who reported to him. On Sunday, Huang wrote a letter to his staff acknowledging he had been unfaithful to his wife and apologizing for breaking his family's and his employees' trust. Days later, the board was preparing to remove him, and on Thursday the resignation was announced.

Here's what the company said happens next. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress will serve as interim CEO while keeping her job as CFO. The board is starting a search for a permanent successor and will consider candidates from inside and outside the company. The board also appointed longtime director Harvey C. Jones as its first-ever chair — Nvidia had never had a board chair before. Nvidia still plans to report its earnings next Tuesday, as scheduled.

This is a big deal because Huang wasn't just any CEO. He co-founded Nvidia in 1993, starting the company with two friends and $40,000 at a Denny's. He built it into the company whose chips power nearly all the AI work happening in the world right now. He owned about 3.6% of the company and, by the Journal's count, took home about $2.4 billion in pay since 2007. When people in Washington and Silicon Valley talked about the AI race, Huang was the man in the middle of it.

The market noticed. Nvidia shares were down almost 8% in premarket trading Thursday. The Journal reports the company is on track to lose more than half a trillion dollars in market value since its board opened its investigation into Huang.

A few things are worth saying plainly. First, the board had earlier said it had "no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised," and it had expressed confidence in Huang's leadership. That changed fast once the second affair — the one with an employee — was reported by the New York Times on Wednesday. Second, Huang himself said in October that it was important he could be fired and someone else given his job. That turned out to be true.

For folks in the trades and small business, the lesson isn't about chips. It's about this: no company, no matter how big or how important, runs on one person forever. Huang built something extraordinary, and one bad set of choices ended a 33-year run in about a week. Character still counts. It counts at the top of a trillion-dollar company the same way it counts on a jobsite — maybe more, because when the boss loses trust, everybody underneath him feels it.

Nvidia will keep making chips, and the AI boom will keep rolling. But the man who lit the fuse is gone, and whoever takes that chair next inherits a company, a board, and a whole lot of questions.
