# October Prices Rose 0.4%. Here's What That Means for Your Wallet

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 11, 2026

Prices went up again in October — just not as fast as they did in September. The Bureau of Labor Statistics released the October Consumer Price Index on Tuesday, November 10, and the headline number is a 0.4% rise for the month, on a seasonally adjusted basis. September's rise was 0.6%.

That is still a rise. A slower rise is not a fall. If your grocery bill, your rent, or your gas tank cost more in October than in September, this report is why — and it is also why cheaper borrowing is not coming soon.

## What drove it

Housing did most of the work. The index for shelter rose 0.3% in October, and the BLS says shelter accounted for over thirty percent of the whole monthly increase. Rent on a primary residence rose 0.2%. When the roof over your head is the biggest line in the report, every family feels it first.

The core index — prices with food and energy stripped out — rose 0.3%. That is the third straight month at 0.3%. Both numbers came in hotter than economists expected: forecasts polled by Dow Jones were 0.3% for the headline number and 0.2% for core. The overall index now sits at 337.511, measured against the 1982–84 base of 100.

## The number that does not exist this month

Here is the part worth knowing before anyone quotes you an annual inflation rate for October: there isn't one. The BLS did not collect survey data for October 2025 because of a lapse in government appropriations, so it could not build October 2025 indexes — and without that base month, it cannot publish 12-month percent changes for October 2026. The bureau says so plainly in its own release.

So if you see someone quoting an October year-over-year inflation rate, they are quoting a number the BLS never published. The last official annual rates, for September, were 3.7% headline and 2.6% core. October has no official annual figure. That is not a cover-up; it is a hole in the data, and the honest thing is to say so.

## What happens next

The Federal Reserve meets December 8–9, and the next CPI release — covering November — is scheduled for December 10, the day after that meeting ends. That means the Fed will make its December decision without seeing November's prices.

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said the morning the report came out that a 50-basis-point rate hike should be considered in December if core CPI came in around 0.3% or higher. It came in at 0.3% — right on the line he drew. The 10-year Treasury yield touched 5.03% on Tuesday as investors weighed the prospect of a hike.

Plain talk: a half-point hike is on the table, not a cut. If you are a contractor pricing a job, a family refinancing a house, or a small business carrying a line of credit, plan like borrowing stays expensive for a while. Budget for the prices you see today, not the prices you hope for next year.
