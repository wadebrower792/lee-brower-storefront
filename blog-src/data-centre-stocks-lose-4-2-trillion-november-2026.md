# The AI Building Boom Just Lost $4.2 Trillion — and the Builders Are Still Working

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 15, 2026

The companies that build the AI boom just took a beating — on paper. A Financial Times analysis published today reports that shares in the companies supplying the equipment, energy systems, and construction services behind the artificial-intelligence data-centre boom have lost about $4.2 trillion in market value since June.

That is a real number, and it is a big one. The losses have erased nearly half of the $9.4 trillion in market value those companies added over the previous twelve months. The sell-off has spread past the chipmakers to the industrial and infrastructure businesses investors had treated as sure winners from the AI build-out: manufacturers, utilities, and mining companies.

Look at the names. Caterpillar, whose heavy equipment and gas generators go into large infrastructure projects, has lost $214 billion in market value — down 44 percent since its shares hit a record high on June 30. Nuclear power company Oklo has fallen 49 percent, even after signing a contract with Meta in January. Comfort Systems, which installs heating and cooling systems, and Corning, which makes fiber optics, have each lost about half their value. Allient, which makes motion-control products, is down 35 percent from its October record.

Now here is the part the headlines usually bury: the work itself has not stopped. U.S. spending on data-centre construction reached about $96 billion in the twelve months to September, not counting IT equipment and chips, according to the latest government data. Caterpillar reported third-quarter sales of $20.7 billion, up 17 percent from a year earlier, with a record $78 billion backlog and power-generation sales up 75 percent. The orders are real. The buildings are going up. What fell was the stock price, not the work.

So why did the market turn? The reporting points to a few things. Investors are questioning whether the AI spending boom can keep going at this pace, and whether the big tech companies can keep paying for it — much of it with borrowed money. There are worries about pressure on tech companies' cash flow from heavy AI spending, possible local restrictions on new data centres, and tighter interest-rate policy. Siemens cut its 2026 revenue forecast on November 12 after delays in third-party data-centre capacity pushed some expected openings into the first half of 2027.

Let me say this plainly, because it matters. A $4.2 trillion loss in market value is not the same thing as $4.2 trillion of cancelled projects. Market value is what investors think a company is worth today. It can fall by half while the company's backlog is at a record high — and that is exactly what happened with Caterpillar. Stocks are a bet on the future. Backlog is work already sold. Those are two different things, and confusing them is how people get scared out of good decisions or talked into bad ones.

I am not telling you to buy or sell anything. That is not my place, and this is not investment advice. What I am telling you is what the numbers actually say: investors got nervous about the AI trade, the suppliers' shares fell hard, and the construction work underneath it is still running at full speed. If you are in the trades, data-centre construction is still one of the biggest sources of real, paying work in the country right now — $96 billion in a year does not vanish because a stock chart went down.

The lesson is an old one. Hype goes up fast, and it comes down faster. The work — the concrete, the steel, the wire, the generators — moves slower, steadier, and longer. Bet on the work.
