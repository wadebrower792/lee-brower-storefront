# Flu Season Showed Up Weeks Early — and It's Hitting the South Hard

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 14, 2026

Flu season usually waits for winter. This year it did not wait. The flu started spreading in late August and early September, and by now it is running weeks ahead of where it normally sits in November — with the South and the West Coast getting hit hardest.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said so in its latest weekly FluView report, released Friday for the week ending November 7. Seven states are now in the high-activity category — California, Louisiana, Oregon, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, and Washington — up from just two states the week before. Outpatient visits for flu-like illness hit 3.2 percent, above the national baseline of 2.9 percent, which is the level that signals the season is truly underway. And 11.3 percent of flu tests in clinical labs came back positive, up from 9.1 percent the week before.

The CDC now estimates at least 2.6 million flu illnesses, 36,000 hospitalizations, and 2,100 deaths so far this season. Two more children died of flu this week, bringing the pediatric death total to five. Those numbers are preliminary — the CDC says so plainly — and they will be revised as more reports come in.

What is driving it is a familiar virus with a new twist. Nearly all of the flu circulating this season is influenza A, and 97.7 percent of the subtyped samples in the CDC's week 43 report were A(H1N1)pdm09, mostly from a subgroup called subclade D.3.1.1. That is a shift from last winter, when a different strain, H3N2, dominated. The circulating H1N1 has picked up a new mutation called G155E. Early lab findings suggest it may make some strains harder for existing antibodies to recognize, but the CDC says it is too early to know whether the mutation is driving the surge or affecting how well this season's vaccine protects people.

The early start is real, and it is measurable. The Associated Press reported that the flu hospitalization rate in mid-October was the highest seen that early in autumn in more than 15 years. The CDC's own FluSight forecasts predict a likely increase in flu hospital admissions and emergency department visits for the week ending November 21, with new admissions projected between 6,900 and 30,100 — a wide range that shows how much uncertainty is still in the picture.

Now for the part that matters most: the CDC is careful to say that an earlier rise in activity does not necessarily lead to a higher seasonal peak or a more severe season overall. Its preliminary severity assessment still rates this season as low across all age groups. An early flu season is not automatically a bad flu season. It is just early, and it is moving fast.

So what should folks do? The same common-sense things that have always worked. Wash your hands often. Stay home when you are sick so you do not pass it around the jobsite or the family. If you are around older folks, little kids, or anybody with a health condition, take it seriously — those are the people flu hits hardest. And if you have not had a flu shot this season, talk to your doctor or pharmacist about it. This season's vaccines target three viruses: influenza A H1N1, influenza A H3N2, and influenza B.

Thanksgiving is less than two weeks away, and a lot of families will be traveling and gathering. The flu is already in the room. A little care now beats a house full of sick folks later.
