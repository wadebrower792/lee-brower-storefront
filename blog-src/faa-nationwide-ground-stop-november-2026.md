# Every Plane in America Was Grounded Friday — Here's What Happened

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 14, 2026

On Friday afternoon, the Federal Aviation Administration did something it had only done once before in 25 years: it grounded every departing flight in the United States.

The order went out just before 4 p.m. Eastern time on Friday, November 13. No plane was allowed to take off from any U.S. airport until further notice. The ground stop lasted more than four hours before the FAA lifted it at about 8:15 p.m. Eastern. It was the longest nationwide ground stop in 25 years and only the second since the September 11, 2001 attacks.

What went wrong was not a plane, a storm, or a runway. It was the system that talks to the planes.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the trouble started at about 3:36 p.m. Eastern, when air traffic control centers began seeing instability in their radar and communications systems. The cause, according to Duffy, was aging telecommunications equipment — old switches and routers on the system's analog network that got overloaded with data and started sending inconsistent readings. When the controllers could not trust what their screens were telling them, the FAA shut departures down rather than risk flying blind.

"They started to give us inconsistency in the data from those routers, and because of that we shut the system down," Duffy said at the White House on Friday night.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told reporters there was "zero evidence" the outage was a cyberattack, and officials said federal authorities were still investigating. The FAA said it did not lose all radar or telecom, and exceptions were made so medevac flights, life-saving cargo, and law enforcement flights could keep operating.

The scale of it is hard to picture. More than 3,500 airplanes were in the sky when the ground stop was issued. By 8 p.m., fewer than 400 were still flying. On Friday alone, FlightAware counted 9,159 cancellations and 7,190 delays on U.S. flights. Saturday morning the mess kept rolling: by 8:17 a.m. Eastern, airlines had already canceled more than 3,500 Saturday flights, with Delta leading at 640 cancellations — about 20 percent of its schedule.

This was not the first warning. The FAA had a nationwide ground stop in January 2023 when a different computer system — the one that sends safety notices to pilots — malfunctioned. And Reuters reports that communications equipment problems have become increasingly common at air traffic control centers. Friday was simply the biggest failure yet.

If you are flying this weekend, here is the plain truth: check your flight before you leave the house, and expect delays even if your flight is still listed. Airlines are rebooking thousands of people, and it will take days to fully untangle. If your flight was canceled, you are entitled to a refund for a canceled flight under federal rules — ask the airline, and keep your receipts.

There is a lesson here for anybody who builds or runs anything. The FAA's system is old, and everybody who works with it knows it is old. New technology was being added to it, and the old equipment underneath could not carry the load. Duffy put it bluntly: "What we have is an incredibly old air traffic control system." On a jobsite, we call that deferred maintenance, and it always costs more when it finally breaks than it would have cost to fix it right the first time.

Officials said the system should be operating normally in time for the Thanksgiving travel rush. Let's hope they are right — because millions of folks will be counting on it.
