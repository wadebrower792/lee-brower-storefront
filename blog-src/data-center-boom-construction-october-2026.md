# The Data Center Boom Is Carrying Construction — and Squeezing Everyone Else

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, October 30, 2026

If you work construction and you feel like the market is split in two right now, the numbers back you up. The October 2026 economic roundup from Construction Executive, built on data from the Census Bureau, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and Associated Builders and Contractors, tells a two-track story: data centers are booming, and almost everything else is just holding on.

## The boom, in plain numbers

Nonresidential construction spending grew for a fifth straight month in August 2026, but the roundup is blunt about why: it is almost entirely the AI buildout. Data center-related construction spending jumped another 7.5 percent in the month and is up more than 70 percent over the past twelve months. Since March, that growth has run at a 149 percent annualized rate. A separate Pillsbury analysis published October 27, 2026 puts the one-year surge at 73 percent, while spending on other private nonresidential construction fell 5.9 percent over the same period.

That split shows up in the backlog too. The Construction Backlog Indicator stood at 8.7 months in September 2026, up 0.2 months from both the month before and the year before. But contractors with data center work carry an 11.3-month backlog, exactly three months longer than the 8.3 months carried by contractors without it. If you are not on a data center job, your pipeline looks very different from the headline number.

## The squeeze: materials, labor, and margins

The same roundup says construction input prices surged 2.2 percent higher in September and are now up 11.2 percent year over year. Much of that jump came from fuel prices, but tariff-affected inputs like iron and steel and copper wire and cable keep climbing too. For a small contractor, that is not an abstract index. It is the price on the quote you wrote last month no longer holding this month.

Hiring follows the same split. The industry added jobs for a seventh straight month in September, yet the growth is almost entirely nonresidential specialty trade contractors — electricians in particular, who are in high demand and increasingly short supply around data center work. Residential employment, meanwhile, fell year over year. The construction unemployment rate in September was 3.5 percent, and average hourly earnings for construction workers were $41.69.

Pillsbury partner James Bobotek, quoted in the firm's October 27 analysis, put the regional effect plainly: in major data center hubs, contractors are seeing longer equipment lead times, tighter electrical labor, and longer waits for power, which can make other projects slower and more costly. He also pushed back on blaming data centers for the broader slowdown, pointing instead to interest rates, affordability, office vacancy, and post-pandemic oversupply for office, retail, and housing.

## What this means for a working contractor

Three things are worth carrying into your next bid. First, a long backlog is not the same as a healthy market — ask whether your backlog is yours or the data center sector's. Second, material prices moved 2.2 percent in a single month, so a quote with no price-validity window is a risk you are carrying for free. Third, more than one in four contractors expect their profit margins to contract over the next six months, according to the Construction Confidence Index cited in the roundup. That is a warning to price the job in front of you, not the market you wish you had.

The boom is real. So is the squeeze. The contractors who come out ahead will be the ones who read both numbers and bid accordingly.
