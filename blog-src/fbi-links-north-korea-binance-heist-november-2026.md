# The FBI Named North Korea in the Binance Heist. North Korea Says No.

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 3, 2026

The biggest crypto theft on record now has an official accusation attached to it — and a flat denial from the accused. On October 31, the FBI attributed the theft of more than $6 billion from Binance, carried out on or about October 29, to TraderTraitor, a hacking operation the bureau links to North Korea. Days later, Kim Yo Jong, the sister of North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, called the accusation a political provocation and denied it outright. Both statements are now on the record. The evidence underneath them is what investigators are still chasing.

Here is what is confirmed. Funds began leaving Binance hot wallets around 4:20 p.m. ET on October 29, according to blockchain analytics firm Chainalysis, and the unauthorized transfers totaled about $6.4 billion — the largest theft in crypto history. TRM Labs put the loss at $6.36 billion across 10 blockchains. The FBI's alert, issued jointly with the U.S. Secret Service and IRS Criminal Investigation, named 608 Ethereum addresses, 19 Bitcoin addresses, and one Solana address, and asked crypto services to refuse transactions touching those wallets or funds derived from them.

The firms that trace stolen crypto reached the same conclusion before the FBI did. TRM Labs linked the attack to TraderTraitor on October 29, and Chainalysis said on October 30 that it had watched Binance funds mix onchain with money from September's Bitget theft, which had also been tied to North Korea. This is the same group the FBI blamed for the $1.5 billion Bybit hack in February 2025, when it named TraderTraitor five days after that theft and asked services to block the listed addresses. The pattern is familiar to investigators: a major exchange theft, a fast attribution, and a long laundering trail.

The laundering trail is moving fast. Elliptic, another blockchain analytics firm, traced the stolen funds through about 12,000 wallets across 11 blockchains and more than 40,000 transactions in under four days — roughly one new wallet every 25 seconds. The firm put the haul at $6.43 billion and said about $2.85 billion, or 44 percent, has been frozen or seized. That recovery share is strikingly higher than the roughly 3 percent recovered after the Bybit hack, a difference Elliptic and others credit to faster freezes: Chainalysis said freezes began by 5:14 p.m. ET on October 29, less than an hour after the funds started moving. Binance said net withdrawals since it restarted withdrawals on Sunday reached $4.72 billion, though hourly deposits began exceeding withdrawals late on Monday.

North Korea's denial came through state news agency KCNA. Kim Yo Jong accused Washington of blaming North Korea whenever a major cybercrime occurs, and challenged the earlier Bybit attribution as well. Elliptic rejected the denial, telling CoinDesk that its on-chain evidence links the theft to North Korea and supports the FBI's attribution specifically to TraderTraitor. It is worth being plain about what an attribution is: a law enforcement conclusion, backed by blockchain tracing and intelligence, not a court verdict. The FBI's public alert did not publish its underlying evidence, and the denial is a political statement, not a technical rebuttal. Neither side's word settles it alone — the wallet addresses, the transaction trail, and the freeze totals are the checkable facts, and so far the tracing firms' public numbers line up with the FBI's account.

What happens next is a grind, not a finale. The hackers are expected to keep laundering through DeFi and instant swap services, and at least one major swap protocol, THORChain — which moved a large share of the Bybit haul in 2025 — has not committed to acting on the FBI's blocking request. Some of the money is barely moving: as of November 2, more than $1 billion in ether and about $1.42 billion in bitcoin from the theft had seen limited onward movement. For ordinary crypto holders, the lesson is not about picking sides in a geopolitical argument. It is about how fast a theft of this size moves, how much depends on services freezing funds within the first hour, and how little of it any single user can control once the money is gone.
