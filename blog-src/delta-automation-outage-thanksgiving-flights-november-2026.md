# Delta's Computer Glitch Delayed 1,000+ Flights — Days Before Thanksgiving

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 23, 2026

If you're flying Delta this Thanksgiving week, Sunday was a warning shot. A problem with the airline's automated weight-and-balance system delayed more than 1,000 Delta flights on Sunday, November 22 — right as the busiest travel week of the year was getting started.

Here's what happened in plain terms. Before a plane can take off, somebody has to calculate its weight and balance — passengers, fuel, and bags — so the aircraft stays within safe limits and keeps a proper center of gravity. Delta does that math with an automated system called ALIS, the Automated Load Instruction System, planned out of its operations center in Atlanta. On Sunday morning, that system went down.

Delta called it a database issue affecting the system that tracks aircraft-loading information. The airline hasn't said what caused it. With the automation out, ramp crews went back to doing it by hand — manually counting bags and working out load sheets — while IT teams worked to bring the system back. One Delta employee was heard telling a pilot in Boston over the radio that the ramp couldn't load planes because there was no ramp worksheet in the system, so crews were "manually counting bags and doing a report for you guys."

The numbers tell the scale of it. FlightAware showed more than 1,600 Delta flights delayed on Sunday — about half of Delta's mainline schedule — and more than 40 canceled. By Sunday evening, 58 percent of Delta's flights were delayed and about 6 percent canceled. Delta said about 70 percent of the day's disruptions were caused by the tech issue, with the rest down to bad weather and air traffic slowdowns in New York and the Northeast. At Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport — Delta's home hub and the busiest airport in the world — more than 60 percent of departing flights were delayed Sunday evening.

The Federal Aviation Administration briefly issued a ground stop for some flights into Atlanta on Sunday morning, then eased it to a ground delay that was canceled around 11:30 a.m. Eastern. The FAA confirmed on social media that the problem was not related to any FAA air traffic system — this one was Delta's own. Delta said the automation was working again by about 3:30 p.m. Eastern, and the airline spent the rest of the day rebooking passengers and offering meals and hotel rooms where needed.

The timing is what makes this one sting. More than 4 million travelers are expected to pass through Atlanta's airport between November 19 and December 1, according to the airport. And this comes just over a week after a separate technology failure — a bad router swap at a remote FAA site — triggered a nationwide ground stop on November 13 that canceled more than 18,000 flights. Two different systems, two different failures, nine days apart, both hitting the same holiday travel crush.

I'm a builder, and I look at this the way I look at a job site: when the tool you depend on every single day has no working backup, the failure isn't a surprise — it's a schedule. Delta's crews did the right thing going manual, and the planes that flew were safe. But manual bag-counting doesn't scale to 5,000 flights a day, and that's the lesson here for every business, not just airlines: if your whole operation runs through one system, you'd better know what happens the day it goes dark.

If you're flying this week, the practical advice hasn't changed: get to the airport early, keep your phone charged and your airline's app open, and fly early in the day when there are still rebooking options if something breaks. Delta says the system is fixed. The week will tell us whether it stays fixed.
