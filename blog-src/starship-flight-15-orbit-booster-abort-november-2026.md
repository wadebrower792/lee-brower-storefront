# Starship Reached Orbit. Its Booster Didn't Make It Home.

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 14, 2026

SpaceX tried something Friday that nobody has ever tried before: catch both halves of the biggest rocket ever built, on the same flight, back at the launch pad. It got half of it done — and the half that got away did it in spectacular fashion.

Starship Flight 15 lifted off from Starbase in South Texas at 4:47 p.m. Central time on Friday, November 13. The rocket stands 124 meters tall — about 407 feet — and its Super Heavy booster is powered by 33 Raptor engines. This was the 15th test flight of the system, and the most ambitious one yet. The plan was to recover the Super Heavy booster with the launch tower's mechanical arms, called chopsticks, and then do the same thing with the Starship upper stage after it came back from orbit.

The booster part did not go as planned. About two and a half minutes after liftoff, the upper stage separated and lit its own engines. The booster turned around and headed back toward the pad, just as it had done on three earlier flights that ended in successful catches. But late in its descent, the booster detected a problem with its guidance, navigation, and control system — GNC for short — and aborted the catch itself. SpaceX commentator Dan Huot said the booster "aborted itself for a GNC issue that it detected," which sent it to its backup plan: a splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico, offshore from the launch site. It came down about six and a half minutes after liftoff and exploded in a fireball before it hit the water.

SpaceX has not said yet what exactly went wrong with the guidance system. The company has built safety checks into the flight that steer the booster away from the tower whenever the conditions for a catch are not met, and Friday was the first time those checks fired on the new Version 3 design of the Super Heavy booster. Three earlier boosters were caught by the tower, but the upgraded version flown Friday had never been caught before.

The upper stage — the part called Starship itself — did its job. It reached orbit about 275 kilometers (170 miles) above Earth, only the second time a Starship has done that, and deployed all 26 of its Starlink V3 satellites over about 42 minutes. SpaceX confirmed the satellites were in orbit Friday evening.

And the flight is not over. Ship 42 is coasting through a long orbit — about 14 trips around the planet over roughly 22 and a half hours — and SpaceX plans to bring it back to Starbase on Saturday afternoon, around 3:20 p.m. Central time, and try to catch it with the tower arms. If it works, it will be the first time any spacecraft returning from orbit has ever been caught that way.

That is the part worth watching. Catching a booster is already hard. Catching a ship that has been to orbit, survived re-entry, and is coming home at hypersonic speed is harder still. SpaceX has backup plans — splashdowns off Hawaii and Chile — if anything looks wrong during the return, and the craft will fly over Mexico and South Texas on its way in.

Here is the plain way to see Friday: the rocket did the new thing it most needed to do in orbit, and the booster recovery — the part that had started to look routine — failed at its first try with the new design. Saturday's catch attempt will decide whether Flight 15 is remembered as a breakthrough or a near miss.

For folks who build things for a living, there is something familiar in it. You test, something fails, you learn from the failure, and you go again. SpaceX has done that fourteen times before this one. Friday was the fifteenth. The sixteenth will come soon enough.
