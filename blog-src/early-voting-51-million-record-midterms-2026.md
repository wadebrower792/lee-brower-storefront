# 51 Million Ballots Are Already In: What the Early-Voting Record Means — and What It Doesn't

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 1, 2026

More than 51 million Americans had already voted by midday Saturday, October 31, three days before Election Day. That is a record for a midterm election, and it already beats the entire early vote from the last midterms.

The count comes from the University of Florida Election Lab, reported by Scripps News. As of midday Saturday, more than 51.3 million ballots had been cast ahead of Tuesday's election. In the whole 2022 midterm cycle, 45.9 million ballots were cast before Election Day. This year's total passed that mark before the weekend even began, with mail ballots still coming in and early voting sites still open in many states.

## In-person voting is driving the surge

The biggest change is not mail ballots. It is people walking in and voting early in person. More than 28 million voters had already cast ballots in person by Saturday, compared with about 20.5 million in-person early votes across the whole 2022 early-voting period.

Texas shows the surge most clearly. More than seven million Texans had voted early, about 1.5 million more than in the whole 2022 cycle in that state. Georgia told a similar story earlier in the week: its secretary of state's office reported 2,294,275 people had voted early in person by Thursday afternoon, already above the 2022 total of 2,289,951 with a day of early voting still to go.

The Election Lab said the 2026 election continues a broader shift toward voting before Election Day, a trend that sped up during the COVID-19 pandemic and never went back.

## What is on the ballot Tuesday

All 435 seats in the House of Representatives and 35 of the 100 Senate seats are being contested on Tuesday, November 3. Republicans head into the vote with a 53-47 Senate majority and a narrow House majority of 218-214, according to USA Today's live election coverage. Democrats need a net gain of four Senate seats to take that chamber, because the vice president can break a 50-50 tie.

## What the early numbers do not tell you

Here is where plain talk matters: an early-voting record is a record of participation, not a result.

Early totals say how many people have voted. They do not say how those people voted. Ballots are not counted by candidate before Election Day in most states, and the national total says nothing about which candidates the early voters chose.

Party registration figures get quoted a lot this weekend, and they need the same careful reading. In the states that report party registration for early voters, Scripps News put registered Democrats at 43.2 percent, Republicans at 31.7 percent, and independent or unaffiliated voters at 25.1 percent. But not every state releases that breakdown — it covers only about 27 states — and registration is not a vote. A registered Democrat can vote for a Republican, and the other way around.

There is one more caution. A record early vote does not automatically mean a record total turnout. Some of those early voters are people who would have voted on Election Day anyway and simply voted sooner. The Election Day vote still to come will decide whether final turnout actually rises.

## The practical point

If you have not voted yet, check your own state's rules rather than going by national headlines. Early voting windows close state by state, and Election Day polling hours and locations are set locally. The record tells you the country is engaged. Tuesday tells you who won.
