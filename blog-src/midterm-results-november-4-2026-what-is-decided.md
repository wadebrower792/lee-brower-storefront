# The Midterms Are Called a Democratic Sweep. Here's What Is Decided — and What Isn't

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 4, 2026

Election night is over, and the morning-after headlines agree on the shape of it: Democrats won control of Congress in the November 3 midterm elections. That part is a projection, not a certified final count, and the distinction matters — because while the big picture is settled enough for the Associated Press and CNN to call it, several races are still uncalled and the exact margins are still moving as votes are counted.

## The Senate: seven flips

CNN's takeaways report Democrats flipped seven Republican-held Senate seats to take the majority: Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina, and Texas. The party also held all of its own seats, according to the same report.

In Texas, Democrat James Talarico defeated Republican Ken Paxton in what CNN calls the most expensive Senate race in U.S. history, and Democrat Gina Hinojosa defeated three-term Republican Governor Greg Abbott in the governor's race — the first time Democrats had won statewide in Texas since 1994, per CNN. In Kansas, Democrat Adam Hamilton defeated Republican Senator Roger Marshall, the first Democrat to win a Kansas Senate race since 1932, according to USA TODAY. In South Carolina, Democrat Annie Andrews defeated Darline Graham, the sister of the late Senator Lindsey Graham, taking a seat that had been in Republican hands since Strom Thurmond switched parties in 1964. In Maine, Democrat Troy Jackson ousted five-term Republican Senator Susan Collins. In Iowa, Democrat Josh Turek defeated Republican Representative Ashley Hinson in the race to replace retiring Republican Senator Joni Ernst. In Ohio, Democrat Sherrod Brown completed a comeback by defeating Republican Senator Jon Husted, and in North Carolina, former Governor Roy Cooper defeated former Republican National Committee Chairman Michael Whatley.

## The historic firsts

USA TODAY's live coverage lists a set of firsts from the night. In Michigan, Democrat Abdul El-Sayed defeated Republican Mike Rogers to become the nation's first Muslim U.S. senator. In New Hampshire, Democrat Chris Pappas became the first openly gay man elected to the Senate, defeating former Republican Senator John E. Sununu. In Georgia, Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms became the first Black woman elected governor of a U.S. state, according to the Associated Press as reported in the November 4 briefing roundup. In Florida, Republican Byron Donalds became the state's first Black Republican governor. And House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries of New York is on track to become the first Black Speaker of the House, per USA TODAY.

## What is still uncalled

Two Senate races remained undecided early Wednesday, according to USA TODAY: Alaska and Mississippi. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom and Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith were separated by a razor-thin margin, CNN reported, and in Alaska — which uses ranked-choice voting — the count could take days, with only about 72 percent of the vote counted early Wednesday, per the Washington Sun's live updates. In the House, Democrats were projected to hold a majority, but races in western states remained too close to call early Wednesday, USA TODAY reported. So any exact seat total you see this morning is a snapshot of a count that is still moving, not a final tally.

## Why voters moved

USA TODAY's coverage attributes the wave to widespread anger over the cost of living and the Iran war, and its exit-poll reporting found frustration over high prices helped deliver Democratic victories. CNN's analysis adds that Democrats won House races in districts Donald Trump had carried by 20 percentage points two years earlier, and toppled Republican state legislative supermajorities in states like Indiana and Missouri. The president's own response, posted after 10:30 p.m. Tuesday, was that he was not on the ballot and Republicans would have won if he had been, CNN reported. That is his claim, not a fact about the vote.

Congress changes hands in January, and the new majorities will shape spending, trade, and energy policy from there. But nothing in last night's results is a law yet, and nothing still being counted should be treated as decided until the count says so.
