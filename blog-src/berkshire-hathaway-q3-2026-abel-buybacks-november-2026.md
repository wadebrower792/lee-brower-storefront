# Berkshire Hathaway's Profit Fell — and It Spent $9.6 Billion Buying Its Own Stock Anyway

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 8, 2026

Berkshire Hathaway reported its third-quarter results on Saturday, November 7, and the headline is a split one. The businesses earned less money than a year ago — but the company spent more than ever this year buying back its own shares.

Operating earnings, the profit from Berkshire's dozens of operating businesses like insurance, railroads, and utilities, fell about 6 percent to $12.71 billion, down from $13.49 billion in the same quarter last year. Net earnings fell harder, down 28 percent to $22.11 billion from $30.80 billion a year earlier. Revenue, though, rose 11 percent to $105.65 billion — the second straight quarter of double-digit growth. So the company sold more and brought in more money, but kept less of it as profit.

The weak spot was insurance. Insurance profit fell 26 percent in the quarter, and underwriting profit — what an insurer earns from premiums after paying claims and costs — dropped 43 percent to about $1.35 billion. GEICO, the car insurer, was the main drag: its pretax profit fell 45 percent as claims costs rose, advertising spending went up, and prices were cut. Its loss ratio, the share of premiums paid out in claims, rose to 76.6 percent.

## What the buybacks say

While profits slipped, Berkshire repurchased about $9.6 billion of its own stock between July and September — more than double the $4.5 billion it bought back in the second quarter, and the biggest quarterly buyback of the year. It bought about $2 billion more in the first four weeks of October, bringing total 2026 buybacks to roughly $14.4 billion through September. Berkshire made no buybacks at all in 2025.

A company buys back its own stock when its leadership believes the shares are worth more than the price being paid. That is the plain reading of it. Berkshire still holds an enormous cash pile — about $377 billion in cash and Treasury bills at the end of September — so the buybacks are not a stretch. They are a choice about where the money does the most good.

## A new man at the top

This is the first earnings report since the September 18 succession, when Warren Buffett became chairman emeritus, Howard Buffett was elected chairman, and Greg Abel continued as chief executive. Abel, 64, took over as chief executive after Buffett stepped down at the end of 2025, and the third quarter was his third as CEO.

Buffett himself has long warned that quarterly net earnings are a poor way to judge Berkshire, because they include gains and losses on stocks the company is not selling. In this quarter, investment gains fell to $9.4 billion from $17.3 billion a year earlier, which is most of the reason net earnings dropped 28 percent while operating earnings fell only about 6 percent. Over the first nine months of 2026, operating earnings actually rose 8 percent, to $37.0 billion from $34.3 billion.

## The lesson for a working man

There is a practical point here for anybody running a business, even a one-truck operation. Berkshire separates the money its businesses actually earn from the paper gains on its investments, and it judges itself on the first number. A contractor ought to do the same: the profit on the job is the real number. The value of the truck sitting in the yard going up or down on paper is not income, and it is not a loss either, until you sell it.

The second point is about what you do with cash when times are good. Berkshire could have sat on its pile. Instead it put $9.6 billion back into its own shares because its leadership thought that was the best use of the money available. When your own business has cash sitting idle, the question is the same: is there a place it earns more — better tools, paying down debt, or buying back your own time — than it does sitting in the bank?
