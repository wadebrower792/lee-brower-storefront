# Spain Shut Down Over Housing — and the Numbers Explain Why

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 12, 2026

On Wednesday, November 11, workers across Spain walked off the job in a general strike. It was the country's first general strike in more than a decade, and it wasn't about wages in the usual sense. It was about housing — about the fact that ordinary working people can no longer afford a roof over their heads.

The strike was backed by Spain's two biggest unions, UGT and CCOO, which together represent more than a million workers, along with tenants' associations angry about rising rents and evictions. The unions said about 4 million workers took part. People stood picket lines in stormy weather across the country, less than three weeks before a snap general election on November 29.

The numbers behind the anger are hard to argue with. Spain has a shortage of about 750,000 homes, and the country's central bank says that gap is set to grow to more than a million by 2028. The average rent is €1,176 a month — about $1,315. And according to the Youth Council of Spain, that average rent is equal to about 98.7% of a young person's average monthly salary.

Read that again. The average young worker in Spain would have to spend nearly every euro they earn just to pay the average rent — before food, before transport, before anything else. That's not a tight budget. That's a system that doesn't work.

Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez is hoping voter anger over housing helps him in the November 29 election. His government has been dealing with corruption cases around his inner circle and a deadly train crash last month, and housing is the issue he wants the campaign to be about instead.

Now, I'm a builder, so here's what I see in this. A housing shortage is a building problem before it's a political problem. Spain is short three-quarters of a million homes, and the gap is growing. You don't fix that with a speech. You fix it by building — by clearing the path for more homes to get built, faster, at prices working people can actually pay.

It's not just Spain, either. Every country with a housing shortage has the same shape to it: too few homes, rents climbing faster than pay, and young people getting squeezed out of the places they grew up. The strike in Spain is one country's version of a frustration a lot of folks feel here at home, too.

A general strike is a blunt tool. It doesn't build a single house. But four million people don't walk off the job on a stormy Wednesday for nothing. When rent eats a whole paycheck, people notice — and sooner or later, they say so out loud.
