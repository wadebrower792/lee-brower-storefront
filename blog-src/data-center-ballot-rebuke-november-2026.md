# Voters Said No to Data Centers 44 Times Out of 45 — Here's What That Means for the Buildout

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 5, 2026

The biggest construction story out of Tuesday's election wasn't a highway bill or a housing plan. It was voters in town after town saying no to data centers — the giant buildings that power artificial intelligence.

On November 3, voters decided 45 local ballot measures about data centers across 10 states. The side favoring limits or bans led in 44 of them. Only one place went the other way: Harwood, North Dakota, where voters rejected an amendment that would have forced extra review of big industrial projects, including data centers. Those are the unofficial counts from the tracker Data Centers on the Ballot, as reported on November 4.

Add up all the votes and the margin is wide. The restriction side took 185,668 votes to 69,517 for the pro-development side — 72.8 percent of the 255,185 votes cast across those 45 measures. But the fine print matters, and I'm going to give it to you straight.

## Not every "no" was the same kind of no

The 44 measures that went against development were a mixed bag. Thirty-four were binding votes — real limits that local governments have to follow. Ten were advisory votes, which tell local officials what residents want but don't force their hand. And the measures themselves ranged from outright bans to temporary moratoriums, caps on how much electricity a facility can draw, and rules requiring voter approval before construction.

Ohio was the center of it. Twenty-one of the measures were in Ohio towns and cities. In Sunbury, Ohio, 78 percent of voters backed a ban on facilities drawing more than 25 megawatts — a vote that halted an Amazon-backed project reported at $2 billion. Voters also backed limits in California, Illinois, Kansas, Maine, New Jersey, Nevada, Florida, and Wyoming, according to Light Reading's count.

## Why this matters to anybody who builds things

Here's the part that hits home for folks in the trades. Data centers are some of the biggest construction jobs in the country right now — billions of dollars of concrete, steel, electrical, and mechanical work. But a New Street Research analysis cited this week says local opposition blocked or delayed about $198 billion worth of U.S. data center projects in the first half of 2026 alone. And a tracking poll the firm cited says public opposition to data centers rose from 42 percent in August 2025 to 75 percent in August 2026.

That doesn't mean the work disappears. Analysts quoted in the coverage say the votes mostly affect future projects planned for 2028 and beyond, not the ones already under construction or approved through 2027. But the message from voters is plain: if a data center is coming to your town, the neighbors now expect a say in it — and in most places this week, they used that say to slow it down or stop it.

The politics crossed party lines in an interesting way. An NBC exit poll found 56 percent of voters called AI and data centers a crucial or major factor in their vote. In Texas, voters elected Gina Hinojosa governor after she promised tougher rules for data centers. In Georgia and Ohio, candidates who campaigned for moratoriums won governorships too.

## What it doesn't prove

One honest caution: these ballots were about data centers in specific towns, not about artificial intelligence in general. The votes don't tell us whether people oppose AI itself, or whether their worries were about power bills, water use, noise, or something else — the reporting doesn't break down voters' reasons. A vote to cap a building's electricity draw is not the same as a vote against the technology inside it.

Still, for anyone who works construction or follows the building economy, this is a shift worth watching. The AI buildout is the biggest construction boom of the decade, and it just met its newest constraint: the ballot box. The projects that get built next will be the ones that win over the neighbors first.
