# COP31 Opens Tomorrow in Antalya — the "Implementation COP" With a First-Ever Shared Presidency

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 8, 2026

The 31st United Nations climate conference, COP31, opens tomorrow, Monday, November 9, in Antalya, Türkiye, and runs through November 20 at the Antalya Expo Center. More than 190 countries are due to take part. If you work in construction, energy, or anything tied to how buildings get built and powered, this one is worth watching — because the agenda is less about new promises and more about turning the promises already made into actual work.

## A presidency split in two

This COP is run differently from every one before it. Türkiye holds the presidency and the venue, with Murat Kurum, Türkiye's minister of environment, urbanisation and climate change, serving as COP31 President. Australia leads the formal negotiations, with Chris Bowen, Australia's climate and energy minister, serving as President of Negotiations. The arrangement was agreed at COP30 in Belém, Brazil, after the two countries had rival bids to host. It is the first shared presidency in the roughly 30-year history of the talks.

A World Leaders' Summit is scheduled for November 11 and 12, in the first days of the talks. A pre-COP meeting was held October 5 to 8 in Fiji, with a leaders' event in Tuvalu.

## What is actually on the table

Organizers have billed this as the "implementation COP." The United Nations climate chief, Simon Stiell, has put it plainly: less about new promises, more about keeping the ones already made. The five priorities named ahead of the conference are accelerating electrification, turning climate commitments into bankable projects, mobilizing public and private finance, strengthening climate adaptation, and protecting oceans. Food security, resilient cities, methane and waste reduction, and green industrialization are also on the list.

The presidency's flagship goal is electrification: raising electricity's share of the world's final energy use to 35 percent by 2035.

The hard numbers explain why the mood is sober. A United Nations synthesis published October 27 looked at the 127 new national climate plans submitted by 154 countries, covering about 71 percent of 2019 global emissions. Those plans point to 2035 emissions averaging about 6 percent below 2019 levels. A pathway consistent with holding warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius needs a cut of about 60 percent. The gap between 6 and 60 is the whole story of this conference.

## The money fight

The central negotiating file is climate finance — who pays for adaptation in countries that did not cause the problem and cannot absorb the costs. The old promise, $100 billion a year from developed nations, was made in 2009 and never fully delivered. The replacement framework, called the New Collective Quantified Goal, has been under negotiation since 2021 and still has no agreed shape as the talks open. A United Nations Environment Programme report released November 4 estimated that tripling adaptation finance would bring it to $85 billion to $120 billion a year, against estimated needs of $305 billion.

On November 3, Brazil's COP30 presidency published the first global roadmap for a transition away from fossil fuels, concluding that every country can start now, whatever its starting point. That roadmap is voluntary, but it sets the terms of the argument in Antalya.

One notable absence: the United States' withdrawal from the Paris Agreement took effect on January 27, 2026, after the U.S. formally notified the United Nations a year earlier. The world's largest economy is not at the table as a party to the agreement.

## What it means on the ground

For a working contractor, the honest read is this: conferences do not build anything, but the money and rules that come out of them eventually do. Electrification targets mean panel upgrades, heat pumps, and grid work. Resilient cities mean storm-hardened buildings and drainage. Bankable projects mean funded jobs with real budgets behind them. The promises are cheap; the follow-through is where the work is. Watch what gets funded in the next two weeks, not what gets applauded.
