# "Hallucination-Free" Legal AI? Stanford Tested It. It Isn't.

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 10, 2026

If you sell a lawyer a research tool, the one thing it absolutely cannot do is make up the law. A fake case in a court filing isn't a typo. It's a sanction. It's a fine. It's a call to the bar.

So when the big legal research companies started selling AI tools, they made a big promise. One said its technology was "eliminating" hallucinations. Another said it was "avoiding" them. A third guaranteed "hallucination-free" legal citations.

Stanford tested those promises. The promises lost.

The study is called "Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools." It comes from Stanford's RegLab and Human-Centered AI institute, and it was preregistered — meaning the researchers wrote down their test before they ran it, so nobody could move the goalposts afterward. Here's what they found, in their own words: "We demonstrate that the providers' claims are overstated. While hallucinations are reduced relative to general-purpose chatbots (GPT-4), we find that the AI research tools made by LexisNexis (Lexis+ AI) and Thomson Reuters (Westlaw AI-Assisted Research and Ask Practical Law AI) each hallucinate between 17% and 33% of the time."

Read that number again. Seventeen to thirty-three times out of a hundred, the tools built specifically for legal research — the expensive ones, the ones with retrieval systems designed to stop exactly this problem — gave back false or misleading information. Invented cases. Citations to rulings that don't exist. Quotes from opinions nobody ever wrote.

And this isn't theory. There is a public database kept by researcher Damien Charlotin that tracks court decisions where a judge found a party relied on AI-fabricated material. I read it today. The count stands at 2,289 cases, updated November 10, 2026. New ones land almost every day. Just this month: a California case on November 6 where a lawyer filed fabricated case law and got a $5,000 fine plus a bar referral. A federal case on October 23 where lawyers filed fake citations and got a $5,000 sanction, a bar referral, and an order to take more legal education. These aren't amateurs. These are licensed lawyers who trusted the tool and didn't check.

What does this mean for a working person? It means the rule is simple and it never changes: check every citation against the real thing yourself. AI can find you a starting point. It can summarize a real case fast. But a fluent, confident answer tells you nothing about whether the case exists. The machine doesn't know when it's guessing. It guesses in the same calm voice it uses when it's right.

And if you ever use AI for anything where being wrong costs money, freedom, or a court date — a contract, a filing, a legal letter — verify every single authority before your name goes on it. The court doesn't care that the bot was confident. It cares that the case was fake. And the fine lands on you, not on the software company.

*Sources: Magesh et al., "Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools," arXiv:2405.20362 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20362 (read Nov 10, 2026). Damien Charlotin, AI Hallucination Cases database — https://www.damiencharlotin.com/hallucinations/ (2,289 cases, read Nov 10, 2026).*
