# 2,061 Gigawatts of Power Are Stuck in Line — and the Line Is Five Years Long

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 16, 2026

America has more power plants and batteries waiting to plug into the grid than it has power plants already running. That's not an opinion. That's the count from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, and the number is big enough to stop you in your tracks.

Berkeley Lab's *Queued Up: 2026 Edition*, published in June 2026 with data through the end of 2025, found about 2,061 gigawatts of generation and storage actively seeking a grid connection across the United States. That's spread over roughly 8,200 projects: 1,312 gigawatts of generation and 749 gigawatts of storage. For scale, the country's entire installed generating fleet is about 1,374 gigawatts — so the waiting line is about one and a half times everything already built.

Here's the part that should bother anybody who builds things for a living. The median project that actually reached commercial operation in 2025 spent 61 months — just over five years — in the interconnection queue, from its first request to the day it started running. Back in 2008, that same trip took 22 months. The line has nearly tripled in length.

And most of what's in line never gets built at all. Of all the capacity that entered queues between 2000 and 2020, only about 13 percent had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025. Three-quarters of it withdrew. The queue isn't a waiting list for projects that are all going to happen. It's a funnel, and most of what goes in comes back out.

What's in the line tells its own story. Solar is the biggest piece at 773 gigawatts. Storage — batteries, 99 percent of it — is right behind at 749 gigawatts. Wind sits at 220 gigawatts. And natural gas, the one that moved the most, jumped 86 percent in a single year to 253 gigawatts. Solar, wind, and storage each fell 16 to 19 percent from the year before, while gas surged. The queue peaked at roughly 2,290 gigawatts at the end of 2024, then fell to 2,061 by the end of 2025 — a 10 percent drop, mostly because more than 750 gigawatts withdrew during the year.

There is a chunk of the line that is closer to real. About 549 gigawatts already holds a draft or executed interconnection agreement and still isn't operating — 256 gigawatts of solar, 161 of storage, 76 of wind, and 45 of gas. Those projects have finished their studies and signed their paperwork. They're still waiting on construction, equipment, financing, or a place in the schedule.

The rules changed to try to fix this. FERC Order No. 2023, effective November 2023, scrapped the old first-come-first-served serial study model for a "cluster study" approach — batch the requests, study them together on a 150-day clock, and stop restudying the whole line every time one project drops out. Whether that actually closes the gap is still the open question for 2027.

Why does any of this matter to a working contractor? Because a 2,061-gigawatt waiting line is a work backlog measured in transmission lines, substations, switchyards, and data centers. Somebody has to build the wires, pour the foundations, set the transformers, and run the conduit. The bottleneck isn't the batteries or the panels anymore — the equipment exists. The bottleneck is the grid connection itself, and every gigawatt stuck in that line is a job that hasn't broken ground yet.

The power is coming. The question is whether the grid can let it in.
