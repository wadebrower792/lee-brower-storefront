# Protesters Climbed the Cranes at a Microsoft Data Centre Site in Amsterdam

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 6, 2026

The backlash against the AI data-center buildout went from the ballot box to the construction site on Friday. More than 100 activists broke into the construction site of a Microsoft AI data centre in Amsterdam, marching in arm in arm, climbing cranes, hanging balloons, and dancing while police stood by. Some carried placards reading "Screw your datacentre." It was the opening act of two days of rallies planned in 16 countries by the "Stop Dirty Data Centres" coalition, which is pulling together environmental groups and digital-rights campaigners behind a call to pause large-scale construction.

The Amsterdam site belongs to Pure Data Centres, which plans to invest more than $1 billion in the project and is backed by Brookfield-owned Oaktree. The company said it was committed to engaging with local stakeholders and asked those on site to leave safely and talk. Microsoft said it understood people had strong feelings and respected their right to express them peacefully, but did not condone unlawful actions.

More actions were planned across the weekend: a march in Dublin, a protest outside the parliament building in Warsaw, and a demonstration against a different Microsoft data centre in Denmark.

## What the protesters want

The campaigners' case is plain: data centres pressure electricity and water supplies, compete with housing and other businesses for scarce land and power, and threaten climate goals. They also point to job losses and to the risks of private companies and big tech concentrating power. The coalition says it is not anti-progress and is not calling for existing data centres to close — it wants major construction halted until governments establish clearer rules.

The tech companies' case is also plain: data centres are essential infrastructure for artificial intelligence and cloud computing, serving businesses, governments, and ordinary people. Europe's tech chief, Henna Virkkunen, told Reuters on Thursday that Europe needs more data centres to power AI development, but added it has to be done with care — citizens should not face skyrocketing energy bills or blackouts because of data centres, and the projects should not destroy the environment.

## This is not one country's fight

The pushback is spreading. A Google project in Finland faces a criminal probe over land clearances. Italy and Portugal are tightening environmental regulation for data centres. Microsoft pulled out of a UK data centre project that was hit by infrastructure delays. The Netherlands alone has about 200 data centres either planned, under construction, or in operation.

And this is the same week American voters weighed in at the ballot box. On November 3, voters decided 45 local data-center measures in the United States, and the restriction side led in 44 of them — I wrote about that earlier this week. Now the same argument is playing out on a construction site in Amsterdam, in the streets of Dublin and Warsaw, and in the markets, where chip stocks fell hard on Thursday in part over fears the buildout could slow.

For a builder, this is worth watching for a practical reason: data centres are some of the biggest construction jobs going, and the rules around them are being written right now — by voters, by regulators, and by protesters on the cranes. A job that looks solid on paper can get a lot harder when the town, the power company, and the water supply all become part of the argument. The work is not going away. But the days of building the biggest box you can, wherever you can, are ending.
