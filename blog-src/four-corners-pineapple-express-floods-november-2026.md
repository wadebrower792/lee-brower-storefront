# A "Pineapple Express" Just Flooded the Desert — and Rivers Are Still Rising

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 3, 2026

It sounds backwards: a desert flooding. But over the weekend, a plume of tropical moisture from near Hawaii — meteorologists call it a "pineapple express" — soaked Arizona and the Four Corners region, and the rivers that normally run low through dry country surged past their banks, forced evacuations across four states, and set records that forecasters said would take days to fall.

The numbers tell the story. The National Weather Service in Flagstaff said radar and rain gauges showed a widespread 2 to 5 inches of rain, and locally more, had fallen since Sunday across and south of the Mogollon Rim, with some stream gauges exceeding record stage heights. The Salt River near Roosevelt rose above its flood of record, peaking at 30.6 feet in the 24 hours ending at 1:30 a.m. Monday. The San Juan River at Pagosa Springs, Colorado, reached 16.81 feet at 8 p.m. Sunday — and officials there had warned it could rise above 20 feet, past roughly 17.8 feet during the historic 1911 flood. More than 10 rivers, creeks, and washes in Arizona and southwest Colorado surged past major flood stage, the highest level, and several set new record crests.

Arizona's governor declared a statewide state of emergency on Monday, November 2. In Sedona, Oak Creek forced evacuations, and crews used boats and helicopters to rescue about 40 people from their homes. State Route 89A through Oak Creek Canyon closed after floodwaters pushed boulders and debris onto the road and washed out sections of pavement. In the Rimrock and Lake Montezuma area, an estimated 150 to 200 homes flooded. In Camp Verde, more than 100 homes along the Verde River were underwater on Sunday night. In Archuleta County, Colorado, more than 1,000 homes were under mandatory evacuation orders, many of them also evacuated during the previous week's storm — this is the region's second major flooding disaster in less than two weeks.

The human cost is already real. In Flagstaff, a 73-year-old man was found dead early Monday after reportedly falling into floodwater Sunday evening, according to the Flagstaff Police Department. The flooding also reached into Election Day itself: a voting center and ballot drop box in Pagosa Springs were relocated, according to the Colorado Secretary of State's office, and Arizona's secretary of state directed affected voters to a dedicated Navajo Nation voting information page. Local officials in San Juan County, New Mexico, and in Coconino and Yavapai counties in Arizona said Monday afternoon that they were not expecting major Election Day disruptions — but voters in flooded areas should check their polling place before heading out, because locations changed.

There is a construction lesson buried in this, and it is one every tradesman in dry country already knows: desert ground does not soak up a sudden 2 to 5 inches. Saturated ground sheds water into washes and rivers that go from dry to dangerous in hours, and roads, culverts, and low crossings built for ordinary rain become the failure points. The rain ended early Monday across much of the Southwest, but flooding along some rivers was expected to last through at least Tuesday, and there was no timetable for fully restoring the roads. U.S. 64 was closed near Chama, New Mexico, U.S. 70 was closed at the Arizona-New Mexico line, and a landslide near the Monticello airport in Utah closed U.S. 191. Recovery here is not just pumping out houses — it is rebuilding roadbeds, clearing debris, and re-checking every crossing before it reopens.

If you live anywhere near this region, the practical advice is simple and comes from the officials on the ground: do not drive through flooded roads, do not walk into floodwater — it takes only inches of fast-moving water to knock a person down and carry a vehicle — and check current road and evacuation status before traveling, because conditions changed overnight and are still changing. The rivers will fall. The roads will take longer. And the second storm in two weeks is a reminder that "historic" flooding is happening often enough now that it cannot be treated as a once-in-a-lifetime event.
