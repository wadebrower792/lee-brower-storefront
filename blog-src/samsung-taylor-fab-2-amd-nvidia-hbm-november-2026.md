# Samsung's Second Texas Plant Is Reportedly for AMD and Nvidia — and It's a Monster Build

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 3, 2026

Samsung Electronics will reportedly use its second foundry plant in Taylor, Texas, to produce base dies for high-bandwidth memory chips for AMD and Nvidia, according to South Korea's Maeil Business Newspaper, as reported by Reuters on Tuesday, November 3. Construction is due to start formally this month. The plant has reportedly secured orders for its entire output, most of it for AMD. Samsung declined to comment. That last detail matters: this is a report sourced to industry sources, not a Samsung announcement — but the scale of what is described is worth understanding either way.

Start with what the plant is for. High-bandwidth memory, or HBM, is the fast memory stacked next to AI processors — the part that feeds data to the chip doing the thinking. A base die is the bottom layer of that stack, the piece that connects the memory to the processor. Taylor Fab 2 will reportedly make those base dies and pair them with DRAM core dies produced in South Korea. The current HBM4 technology uses base dies built on a 4-nanometer process; Fab 2 is aiming for 2-nanometer technology or even more advanced. In plain terms, the plant is not making finished chips you would recognize. It is making one critical layer of the memory that AI hardware runs on, for the two companies whose names dominate that market.

Now the size. The plant will cover about 2.7 million square feet, roughly 1.5 times the size of Samsung's first Taylor facility, according to Maeil. Equipment is expected to be installed from February 2029, with mass production in 2030. That timeline is the part that should keep everyone's expectations honest. A formal construction start this month does not mean chips next year. It means years of sitework, concrete, clean rooms, and equipment installation before a single production wafer runs. Anyone who has worked a big industrial job knows that rhythm: the groundbreaking is the easy press release; the next three years are the actual job.

The first Taylor plant sets the context. It is still under construction and has a contract with Tesla — a $16.5 billion agreement signed in July 2025 for Samsung to manufacture Tesla's next-generation AI chips there. Fab 1 is expected to reach full operational capacity by the end of 2026, then ramp toward production in 2027. So Taylor is already one of the biggest semiconductor construction stories in the country, and Fab 2 would roughly double down on it, with its output reportedly already spoken for before the walls go up.

For construction folks, this is the kind of project that reshapes a region's labor market for years: thousands of skilled tradespeople, a long runway of steady work, and suppliers and subcontractors stacked behind the main build. For everyone else, it is a useful reminder of where the AI boom actually lives. The headlines are about chatbots and stock prices. The physical reality is concrete, steel, clean rooms, and water and power infrastructure, built years ahead of the products they will feed. A memory component plant slated for 2030 production is a bet that AI hardware demand is still climbing four years from now — a bet Samsung's reported customers have apparently already placed with their orders.

One caution before the excitement runs away: Samsung has not confirmed the AMD and Nvidia details, and the report rests on unnamed industry sources. What is solid is that a second Taylor plant is coming, that it is very large, and that its timeline runs to 2030. The customer list is reported, not announced. Treat it that way, and the story still stands on its own: Texas is about to host one of the largest chip-building efforts in the country, and the AI industry's supply chain is being poured in concrete years before it is needed.
