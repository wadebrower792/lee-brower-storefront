# Tomorrow, a Spacecraft Arrives at Mercury After Eight Years

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 20, 2026

Tomorrow, Saturday, November 21, 2026, a spacecraft that left Earth eight years ago is scheduled to do something only one mission has ever done before: slip into orbit around Mercury, the planet closest to the sun.

The spacecraft is BepiColombo, a joint mission of the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA). It launched on an Ariane 5 rocket on October 20, 2018, and it has spent the years since flying a long, looping path through the inner solar system, using flybys of Earth, Venus, and Mercury itself to slow down enough to be caught by Mercury's gravity.

## Two orbiters, one arrival

What makes this arrival different from every Mercury mission before it is that BepiColombo is really two spacecraft flying as one. ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, called Mio, are still stacked together for the capture. When they separate on December 9 and 10, BepiColombo will become the first mission ever to put two spacecraft in orbit around Mercury at the same time — and only the second mission ever to orbit the planet at all. The first was NASA's MESSENGER, which orbited Mercury from 2011 to 2015.

The arrival itself is not one big engine burn. ESA describes the approach as a "weak stability boundary capture": the spacecraft is steered to a chosen point near Mercury, the planet's gravity takes a loose hold of it, and then the MPO's chemical thrusters fire to turn that loose hold into a proper orbit. From there, a sequence of 16 maneuvers runs over about six months. The MPO reaches its final science orbit — 480 by 1,500 kilometers above the surface, circling the planet about every 2.3 hours — on March 10, 2027, and the main science phase begins April 6, 2027.

## A trip that ran late — and why

BepiColombo was supposed to arrive in December 2025. It is arriving eleven months late because of a power problem found in April 2024: unexpected electrical currents between the solar arrays and the power unit cut the thrust available from the spacecraft's ion engines. The flight team rebuilt the route around the weaker thrust, and the mission's transfer module — the part that carried the two orbiters across the solar system — separated on September 3, 2026. That is the kind of fix that never makes headlines: no new rocket, no rescue mission, just engineers reworking the math until the old plan still worked.

The numbers tell you how far away this is happening. BepiColombo will be about 150 million kilometers from Earth when it is captured, so a radio signal takes more than eight minutes to travel from the spacecraft to ESA's control room in Darmstadt, Germany. Nobody on Earth will be flying it in real time. The burn is planned, the spacecraft executes it, and the team waits for the signal to come back and tell them it worked. ESA has published no clock time for the capture, and the agency cautions that exact dates can still change for operational reasons.

## Why Mercury is worth the trouble

Mercury is the smallest planet and the hardest to reach, not because it is far — it is the nearest planet to the sun — but because the sun's gravity makes it brutally hard to slow down enough to stay there. That difficulty is also the reason scientists want to go: Mercury's oversized iron core, its magnetic field, and its scorched, cratered surface hold clues about how the rocky planets, including Earth, formed. Two orbiters working at once — one studying the surface and interior, the other studying the magnetic environment — can answer questions one spacecraft alone could not.

Eight years, nine flybys, one power failure, and a rebuilt flight plan — and tomorrow the long trip ends with a planet catching its visitor. If it works, the real science starts in April. If the date slips, the team tries again. Either way, it is worth watching: this is the farthest thing from a same-day delivery in human history, and it is arriving right on a rebuilt schedule.
