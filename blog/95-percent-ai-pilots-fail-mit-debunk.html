"95% of AI Pilots Fail"? That's Not What MIT Said.

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 10, 2026

You've heard this number. It's been in a thousand headlines, a thousand LinkedIn posts, a thousand sales decks. "95% of AI pilots fail." It sounds final. It sounds like science. It sounds like the whole AI thing is a bust.

It isn't what MIT said. I read the report myself, and the headline is a misread.

The report is called "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025." It comes from MIT's Project NANDA, published in July 2025. Here's what it actually says, word for word: "Despite $30–40 billion in enterprise investment into GenAI... 95% of organizations are getting zero return."

Organizations. Not pilots. Organizations.

Here's the funnel from the same report, the part the headlines leave out: "Sixty percent of organizations evaluated such tools, but only 20 percent reached pilot stage and just 5 percent reached production."

Now do the division the headline never does. Twenty percent reached the pilot stage. Five percent reached production. Five out of twenty is one in four. Among the organizations that actually ran a pilot, one in four got it into production and making a real difference. That's a 75% failure rate among pilots — still bad, but it is not 95%.

The 95% counts everybody, including the roughly 80% who never ran a custom pilot at all. That's like saying 95% of people fail at marathon running, when most of them never entered a race.

And there's more the headline hides. The report calls itself "Preliminary Findings" right on the cover. It rests on 52 interviews and 153 survey responses collected at industry conferences — a small, non-random sample, by the authors' own description. It grades success six months after a pilot, and its own authors admit six months "may be insufficient," because large-company rollouts take nine months or longer. The report even warns its figures are "directionally accurate based on individual interviews rather than official company reporting."

So what does this mean for a working person? Two things. First, don't let a scary number talk you out of using AI where it actually works. A tool that saves you an hour a day on real work is worth having, no matter what a headline says about enterprise pilots. Second, don't let the number's failure fool you in the other direction either. The report's real finding is solid and worth hearing: most AI tools stall because they don't learn your workflow, don't remember context, and don't fit how the work actually gets done. The tool isn't the problem. The fit is.

Read the funnel, not the headline. The truth is in the middle, and it's more useful than the slogan.

Source: MIT Project NANDA, "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" (July 2025) — https://mlq.ai/media/quarterly_decks/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf (read Nov 10, 2026).
