South Korea Will Pay You $375,000 to Catch an AI Exam Cheat

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 24, 2026

South Korea is now offering cash rewards of up to 500 million won — about $370,000 to $375,000 — to anyone who turns in a student caught cheating on the country's college-entrance exam with artificial intelligence. It's one of the biggest whistleblower bounties ever put on school cheating, and it tells you just how far AI has gotten ahead of the people trying to police it.

Here's how the reward works, according to South Korea's Anti-Corruption and Civil Rights Commission. A tipster gets 100 million won, roughly $75,000, for each tip that leads investigators to a cheating case — including if the tipster was a cheater themselves. The payouts can be awarded for up to five referrals that result in a guilty verdict, which is how the total reaches 500 million won. The commission is accepting reports through January 19 as part of a two-month campaign that started November 20.

The exam in question is the College Scholastic Ability Test, known as the Suneung, held this year on November 19. More than 550,000 students sat for it across 1,312 testing centers, and the stakes could hardly be higher — in South Korea, that one eight-hour test shapes university admissions and, for a lot of families, a young person's whole future. Education officials recorded 111 violations involving smart devices during this year's exam. Students are hiding tiny cameras and earpieces, photographing test questions, and getting answers back from generative AI within seconds.

The crackdown didn't come out of nowhere. On November 15, a 34-year-old man named Han Won-seok confessed on his lawyer's YouTube channel that he had cheated his way to first place on the country's certified tax accountant exam, scoring 397.5 out of 400 points in April using a miniature camera, generative AI, and a smartphone. Days later, allegations surfaced that another man who earned a perfect score on the Legal Education Eligibility Test had used AI to cheat too. President Lee Jae-myung personally pushed the reward ceiling up — twice, after officials first announced a 100 million won cap that he said fell short of his directive.

The fallout is already reaching the top. South Korea's education minister resigned on Sunday, saying he was too old to tackle the country's AI test-cheating problem. Students caught cheating face being barred from taking the exam for 10 years.

Now, there's a real question underneath the headline number. When a government starts paying $75,000 a head for tips about school cheating — and paying the cheaters themselves if they turn in their friends — it means the old way of stopping it has already failed. Cameras and proctors can't catch a device the size of a button. The test is eight hours long, taken once a year, and the pressure on these kids is enormous. The bounty might catch some cheaters. It won't fix the reason kids feel they have to cheat in the first place.

For anybody building or selling AI tools, this is worth watching closely. The same technology that writes your emails and drafts your bids can also read a photographed test question and solve advanced math in seconds. That power is real, and it's already out in the world — in classrooms, on jobsites, everywhere. The question isn't whether AI will be used. It's whether we use it honestly. A $375,000 bounty says one country has decided the answer matters enough to pay for it.
