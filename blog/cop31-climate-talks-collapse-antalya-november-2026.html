COP31 Just Collapsed in Turkey — the First Climate Summit to Break Down Since 2009

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 22, 2026

The world's biggest climate summit just ended with no deal at all. COP31, the 31st Conference of the Parties to the U.N. Framework Convention on Climate Change, collapsed on Sunday in Antalya, Turkey, after more than two weeks of talks — the longest climate summit in history, and the first major breakdown since COP15 in Copenhagen in 2009.

The conference opened November 9 and was supposed to close November 20. Instead, negotiators pushed two days past the scheduled end, and at the final meeting meant to approve the so-called Antalya Pact, there was no consensus among the more than 190 countries at the talks. COP31 president Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister, told the closing session that some parties objected and the decisions therefore had not been adopted. The United States was not among the countries at the table — under President Donald Trump, the U.S. has pulled out of international climate action.

The breaking point was the very cause of climate change itself: fossil fuels. The first draft of the COP31 "cover decision" — the overarching agreement from the conference — was published the week of November 16 and called for "the immediate transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems." But every mention of fossil fuels was stripped out of every subsequent draft, despite a push by more than 100 countries to keep the language in. By the fourth and final draft, released Sunday, the text only "recalled" the deal from COP28 in Dubai in 2023, when countries agreed for the first time to "transition away from fossil fuels."

That was the red line. The European Union, the United Kingdom, Panama, the Marshall Islands, and Norway were among those that rejected the final text. UK energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh called the failure "immensely disappointing" and said the draft "moves us backwards on the science." Darragh O'Brien, Ireland's transport minister, speaking for the EU during its presidency, said the bloc "will not accept a Cop that greenwashes fossil fuels" and said the draft had been "hijacked" by oil states. Saudi Arabia and other oil-producing nations had repeatedly opposed any explicit reference to reducing fossil fuel use, and Russia said it could not agree to any mention of fossil fuels in the text at all. "This is a red line for us," said Maina Talia, climate minister of Tuvalu, the Pacific island nation. "We're talking about the root cause of all this. Without it we are wasting our time here."

Money was the other fault line. Delegates also failed to agree on increasing financial support for countries already grappling with climate catastrophes. Mohamed Adow, director of the Power Shift Africa thinktank, put it this way: "We came to Antalya needing progress on public climate finance, adaptation and fossil fuels. Instead, we are fighting to stop governments retreating from promises they have already made."

It wasn't a total blank. Chris Bowen, Australia's climate minister and the negotiations president, said there were "a lot of good things" to come out of the talks, including more than 90 countries signing a pledge to ramp up the electrification of their economies. But the main "cover decision" — the agreement that was supposed to define the summit — failed, and that left the summit effectively ending in "no deal."

The collapse also puts a hard fact on the record: the latest draft acknowledged that the goal set by the 2015 Paris Agreement — keeping the rise in global temperatures below 1.5 degrees Celsius — is going to be missed.

What happens next is unclear. Kurum could convene a follow-up meeting, or the decisions could be delayed until next year's conference, hosted by Ethiopia. The obligations countries already carry under existing international climate accords are not erased by this failure — but no new ground was gained in Antalya.

For working folks watching from the sidelines, the practical read is simple: the world spent two weeks and millions of dollars in a resort city and walked out without agreeing on the one sentence that mattered — that the world needs to move away from the fuels causing the problem. Whether you think these summits matter or not, that failure tells you something honest about where things stand. The next chance to fix it is a year away.
