Let an AI Agent Into Your Bank Account? The Banks Say Whatever Happens Is On You

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 19, 2026

Here is a warning worth hearing before you hand your bank login to an AI assistant. Citibank changed its Online User Agreement this week to say that if you give a third-party AI agent access to your account, you are responsible for whatever that agent does — even if the agent gets hacked and a thief drains your money.

That is not a rumor. TechCrunch reported it on November 18, quoting a Citi spokesperson: "As third-party AI tools become more widely available, we want customers to understand the risks associated with sharing banking credentials or authorizing those tools to access their accounts. We recently updated our Online User Agreement to provide greater clarity about those risks and to reinforce that customers who choose to grant account access to third-party AI agents are responsible for activities conducted through those agents."

Citi's new Section H, on the use of third-party artificial intelligence, covers any outside AI agent used to access accounts through Citi Online or Citi Mobile. The key clause: if the credentials you gave the agent are shared, leaked, or passed along to anyone else, Citi will treat any access and actions taken with them as authorized by you. In Citi's separate Agreement for Online Funds Transfers, the customer acknowledges being "solely responsible for all instructions provided to Citi by any third-party AI agent acting on my behalf." Transfers started by the agent are deemed initiated and authorized directly by the customer, and Citi says it has no duty to examine whether the agent was acting within its authority.

Citi is not alone. JPMorgan and Bank of America, the two largest banks in the country, announced similar policies last month. PNC changed its terms on November 18. Citizens emailed customers this week with a similar warning. United Airlines changed its terms on November 11 to bar unauthorized third-party AI agents from making or changing tickets, reserving the right to cancel bookings they make.

Now, the part the scary headlines leave out. A bank's agreement does not automatically erase federal consumer protections. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's Regulation E says no agreement can impose more liability for an unauthorized electronic transfer than the regulation allows. The open question — and nobody has settled it yet — is whether a transfer made by a hijacked AI agent counts as "unauthorized" at all. If you intentionally gave an agent authority and it went beyond your instructions, your dispute gets much harder. If a third-party service is hacked and a thief moves money without actual authority, that may still count as unauthorized. No regulator or court has decided how the rules apply to agent hijackings.

So what should a working person actually do? Keep it simple. Do not hand your bank login or credentials to a third-party AI tool, no matter how handy it promises to be. If your bank offers its own AI assistant, that is a different thing — Citi's own terms say its own AI tools do not carry the same extra liability. Read the terms before you connect anything to your money. And if an app asks for your banking password to "analyze your spending," that is the moment to stop and think about who pays if it goes wrong. Right now, the banks are telling you plainly: it would be you.

AI tools are useful. I use them every day. But your bank account is not the place to test what an agent might do with your money. Keep the AI on the paperwork and the estimates — and keep your banking credentials in your own hands.
