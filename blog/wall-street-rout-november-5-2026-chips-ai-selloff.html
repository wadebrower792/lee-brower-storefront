Wall Street Gave Back the Whole Rally in One Day — and Chips Took the Worst of It

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 6, 2026

One day after the market celebrated the midterm results, it gave the whole celebration back — and then some. On Thursday, November 5, U.S. stocks fell hard in every direction. The Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 1,833.06 points, or 3.44 percent, closing at 51,523.06. That wiped out Wednesday's 1,569-point gain and then some. The S&P 500 fell 4.36 percent to 7,353.73, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52 percent to 24,315.00, putting the Nasdaq in correction territory — 11.9 percent below its record close from October 6. All three indexes had their worst day since the tariff sell-off in April 2025.

The selling was not spread evenly. It was concentrated in the stocks that had run the furthest on the artificial-intelligence boom. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.25 percent, its second-biggest one-day drop on record, with all 30 of its members down. Micron fell 17.46 percent. AMD fell 17.26 percent. Nvidia, the chipmaker at the center of the AI trade, fell 9.26 percent. SanDisk dropped 22.26 percent, the worst fall in the S&P 500 that day. Qualcomm fell 9.49 percent after its phone-chip sales missed expectations, and memory makers Western Digital and Seagate each fell more than 16 percent. The Roundhill Memory ETF fell 15 percent. Even Tesla fell 11 percent.

Why it happened

The trigger was politics, not a bad earnings season. Investors looked at the Democrats' sweep in the midterm elections — control of both chambers of Congress, plus governors elected in key states — and worried that tighter regulation could slow the data-center buildout that drives demand for AI chips. Of the 34 winning gubernatorial candidates, 32 supported tighter regulation or restrictions on data centers, and 12 backed bans or temporary moratoriums. Higher borrowing costs were also in the mix: the Associated Press reported that investors worried dearer money could slow data-center construction and chip purchases. Add in year-end profit-taking, and the market's hottest trade unwound in a single session.

The scale of the move was large by any measure. Deutsche Welle estimated as much as $2.5 trillion in market value was wiped off the Nasdaq, and the Guardian put the loss at more than $2 trillion. In Asia the slide kept going: Japan's Nikkei 225 had already fallen four straight days, and chipmakers Samsung, SK Hynix, and Tokyo Electron all fell hard on Friday. Reuters noted on Friday that U.S. shares were heading for a second day of sharp losses.

What it means for regular folks

If you have a 401(k) or an IRA, you probably saw the balance dip on Thursday — and the honest thing to say is that one bad day is not a plan, and it is not a reason to panic. Wealth adviser Mark J. White told Fox Business that people should be cautious about making sudden changes to retirement investments in response to market volatility. That is the steady advice in a week like this: the money in a retirement account is meant to ride out years, not days.

The deeper story here is about the data-center buildout itself. The same week American voters were saying no to data centers at the ballot box — I wrote about that earlier this week — investors started pricing in the chance that the buildout slows down. Whether that fear turns out to be right is something nobody can know yet. What is certain is that the AI trade, which carried the market for most of the year, is no longer a one-way ride.
