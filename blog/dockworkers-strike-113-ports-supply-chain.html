Dockworkers Struck at 113 Ports on Friday. Here's What It Means for Materials and Deliveries.

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, October 31, 2026

On Friday, October 30, 2026, dockworkers went on strike across Europe and beyond, disrupting shipping and ferry services in a coordinated action that covered 113 ports in 22 countries and was backed by more than 25 unions. If you order materials, equipment, or anything that crosses an ocean, this is the kind of week that moves your delivery dates — so here is what actually happened, what is confirmed, and what is not.

What happened

The strike was originally called by dockworkers' unions in Italy, France, Greece, Spain's Basque Country, Turkey, and Morocco, and it grew to cover major shipping hubs including Piraeus, Valencia, Le Havre, Algeciras, and Genoa, as well as Morocco's Tangier Med and India's Nhava Sheva. In Greece, ferries stayed docked as dockworkers, engineers, and seafarers' unions joined the action. The strikers protested the use of their ports for arms shipments and the number of seafarers killed in wars, and they also called for pay rises, better working conditions, and doctors aboard vessels crossing war zones.

The casualty figures behind the protest are stark. Organizers counted 570 confirmed attacks in the Black Sea so far this year, leaving 40 seafarers dead and hundreds wounded, and 28 attacks on vessels in the Gulf in the first 21 days of October — a record since the start of the Iran war, according to the unions' count. In May 2026, dockworkers' unions from more than 35 European countries had already published the Istanbul Declaration, demanding that port facilities not be used to transport weapons destined for war zones. Friday's strike was that declaration put into action.

What is not confirmed

One big number needs a flag. A protest group said the action would halt some 52,000 containers and snarl cargo worth $2.8 billion. Reuters reported that figure with an explicit caveat: it was not able to independently verify the impact. A separate pre-strike report said organizers expected the action could disrupt over 40 percent of Mediterranean cargo and snarl over $2 billion in freight. Treat those as organizers' estimates, not measured outcomes — the real disruption will be counted in the days ahead, port by port.

Closer to home: Los Angeles and Long Beach

There is also a separate American port story this week, and it ended differently. The employer group at the Los Angeles and Long Beach seaports and the union representing about 250 maritime watchmen agreed on Friday to extend their negotiating deadline from November 1 to November 5, averting a potential strike at the nation's busiest container seaport complex. The watchmen inspect terminal-gate entries, provide terminal security, and track critical cargo; they work at terminals handling roughly 85 percent of cargo at Long Beach and 33 percent at Los Angeles. Both ports reported all-time cargo volume records for September, with shipments including retail goods for holiday sales, equipment for AI data center construction, and auto parts.

What a contractor should do with this

A one-day strike in Europe does not by itself empty American lumber yards, but it lands on top of record port volumes and a holiday-season shipping surge — and that combination is exactly how lead times slip. If you have materials or equipment on order with an overseas leg, call your supplier this week and confirm the shipment is actually moving, not just scheduled. If you are bidding work that depends on imported fixtures or equipment, put honest lead times in the bid, in writing. And watch the November 5 deadline in Los Angeles and Long Beach: if those talks break down, the busiest container complex in the country becomes the next choke point.
