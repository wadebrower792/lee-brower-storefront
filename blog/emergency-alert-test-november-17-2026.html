Your TV Will Beep at 1:20 Today — It's Only a Test, and Your Phone Won't Ring

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 17, 2026

If you're watching TV or listening to the radio this afternoon, your program is going to stop for about a minute. Don't panic, don't call anybody, and don't believe the posts saying the lights are about to go out.

At 1:20 p.m. Central time today, Tuesday, November 17, the Federal Emergency Management Agency is running a nationwide test of the Emergency Alert System. That's 2:20 p.m. Eastern, 12:20 p.m. Mountain, and 11:20 a.m. Pacific — the whole country at the same moment. The message will say, in plain words, "This is a nationwide test of the Emergency Alert System, issued by the Federal Emergency Management Agency. This is only a test. No action is required by the public." Then your show comes back on.

Here's what you need to know. The test runs on TV and radio only — broadcast stations, cable, satellite TV, and AM, FM, and satellite radio. Your cell phone will not go off. This round does not use Wireless Emergency Alerts, the system that sends alerts to phones. That's different from the last nationwide test in October 2023, which did reach phones. So if your phone stays quiet this afternoon, nothing is wrong. That's how it's supposed to work.

The test is routine, and it's required by law. The IPAWS Modernization Act of 2015 says FEMA has to test the nation's public alert system at least once every three years. The FCC announced this one publicly back on September 16, two months ago. The point is simple: make sure the system that would warn the whole country in a real emergency can actually reach every station. If severe weather or a real emergency gets in the way today, the backup date is Thursday, December 3, at the same time.

Now for the rumor, because it's been all over social media. Posts have been claiming the test is the start of a 90-day nationwide blackout, or that the internet is going to "die" today. There is no evidence for that. As of this morning, no federal agency, no utility, and no grid operator has announced any shutdown. The test doesn't touch the power grid — it checks whether a message can get from Washington to your local TV and radio stations.

Where did the "90 days" come from? There is a real 90-day plan, but it's about military bases, not your house. On September 30, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced an initiative called FORTRESS America, aimed at making military bases able to run on their own power for 90 days if the grid ever went down. That's a military readiness plan. It has nothing to do with today's test, and it doesn't mean a blackout is coming for anybody else.

So here's the plain version. At 1:20 p.m. Central, your TV or radio will pause for about a minute, play a test message, and go back to normal. Your phone won't ring. The lights will stay on. If somebody tells you different, ask them to show you the announcement from FEMA or your power company — because there isn't one.
