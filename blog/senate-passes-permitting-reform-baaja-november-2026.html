The Senate Just Voted 62-33 to Let America Build Faster

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 20, 2026

If you have ever waited on a permit, you know the feeling: the job is ready, the crew is ready, the money is ready — and the paperwork is not. On Thursday night, November 19, the U.S. Senate voted 62-33 to pass a sweeping bill aimed at exactly that problem. It is called the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, or BAAJA, and it now heads to the House of Representatives.

The bill was put together by an unusual coalition: Republican Senators Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia and Mike Lee of Utah, working with Democratic Senators Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island and Martin Heinrich of New Mexico. Fifteen Democrats voted for it. It passed without any additional floor votes on amendments.

What the bill changes

At its core, the bill rewrites how federal environmental reviews work for big projects — power plants, pipelines, transmission lines, mines, highways, and data centers. A White House policy paper estimates that federal permitting requirements for major infrastructure take 4.2 years to complete and produce about 1,700 pages of paperwork. BAAJA is built to cut that down.

The biggest change is to the National Environmental Policy Act, or NEPA, the law that requires federal environmental reviews. Today, lawsuits challenging a NEPA review can be filed up to six years after the fact. Under BAAJA, that window shrinks to 150 days — and only people who took part in the public comment process and can show direct harm can sue. If a judge finds a violation, the project approval generally stays in effect while the agency fixes the problem, with a deadline of no more than 180 days to complete the corrections. Construction keeps moving instead of stopping while the paperwork gets corrected.

The bill also revises the Clean Water Act, the Endangered Species Act, and the National Historic Preservation Act, and it makes it harder for a president to revoke permits that have already been issued. For housing, the bill removes some of the most time-consuming federal review steps for HUD- and USDA-funded projects; industry groups estimate the streamlined water permitting alone could cut related compliance costs by up to roughly $425,000 per project.

The data-center piece

One part of the bill speaks directly to a fight happening in towns across the country. New data centers of 20 megawatts or more would have to pay the full incremental cost of the grid infrastructure built to serve them, so those costs do not land on regular electric customers. That provision answers the worry many voters raised this month — that giant data centers drive up power bills for everybody else.

What it means for builders

Let me be straight about what this does and does not do. This is federal permitting reform. It affects big projects with a federal connection — energy, transmission, pipelines, federally funded housing. It does not change your local building permit, your county inspection, or your state licensing. The permit you pull on Monday morning for a house or a shop is still a local matter.

But if you work on federal jobs, transmission work, pipelines, or large infrastructure — or if you are waiting on a project that has been stuck in federal review for years — this bill is the closest Congress has come in years to fixing that logjam. The House still has to vote, and the Washington Examiner reports the bill faces a steep hill there. Majority Leader Steve Scalise has said he is optimistic Congress will send a bill to President Donald Trump's desk, and the president has endorsed the legislation. Until the House acts, though, it is a Senate vote, not a law — and a builder plans on the law, not the headline.
