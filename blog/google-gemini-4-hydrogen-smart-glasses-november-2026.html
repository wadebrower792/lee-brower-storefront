Google Just Put a New AI Brain Behind Search — and Glasses on Your Face

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 13, 2026

Google had a big day on Thursday, November 12. It launched a new AI model called Gemini 4 Hydrogen, put it behind Google Search for everybody in the world the same day, and opened pre-orders for its first smart glasses with Gemini built in. Two launches at once — and both of them touch the tools a lot of us use every day.

Start with the model. Gemini 4 Hydrogen is Google's fast, low-cost workhorse in the new Gemini 4 family. Google says it runs tasks 2.5 times faster than the model it replaces, Gemini 3.8 Flash. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index it scores 51, one point ahead of its bigger sibling Gemini 4 Argon. For developers the price is $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens.

What matters more to regular folks is where it went. Google deployed Hydrogen in Search for all users worldwide on the same day it was announced, calling it the biggest model update to Search yet. It is also the default in the Gemini app, and it showed up the same day in coding tools like Antigravity, Cursor, and Devin. In plain terms: the answers you get from Google Search now come from a newer, faster AI brain than the one that was there on Wednesday.

Then the glasses. Google's Intelligent Eyewear — designed with Warby Parker and Gentle Monster, engineered with Samsung — opened for pre-order on November 12 and goes on sale December 10. Warby Parker frames start at $395 across three styles — Alden, Reid, and Wren — and Gentle Monster frames start at $449 across six designs. They'll be sold in the United States and South Korea only, for now.

These are audio glasses, not screen glasses — there is no display in the lens. Each pair weighs about 41 grams, runs roughly nine hours on a charge, and carries a 12-megapixel ultra-wide camera, open-ear speakers, and a charging case that refills the frames up to seven times. You talk to Gemini hands-free: ask what you're looking at, hear Google Maps directions in your ear, get a translation of a conversation in real time in more than 70 languages. One feature stands out — Guided Vision, which gives spoken descriptions of what's in front of the wearer, built for people who are blind or have low vision, like reading small print or finding everyday items.

On privacy, Google says camera visuals processed for Gemini are not saved in your Gemini History and aren't used to train AI models or target ads. The glasses require a Google account from the United States or South Korea, and the account holder has to be 18 or older.

Here's the straight talk. A faster model behind Search is genuinely useful — if the answers get better, everybody wins. The glasses are a different question. At $395 to $449 before prescription lenses, they're a want, not a need, and the first version of any new gadget is the one that teaches the company what to fix in version two. If you wear glasses anyway and the price fits your budget, the pre-order is there. If not, the new Search model is already in your pocket for free, and that's the part of this launch that actually changes your day.
