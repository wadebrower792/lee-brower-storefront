The Bank of England Just Raised Rates for the First Time in Three Years

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, November 5, 2026

The Bank of England raised interest rates on Thursday, November 5 — the first time it has done so in more than three years — and it warned that at least one more increase is likely if the war between the United States and Iran keeps going.

The bank's Monetary Policy Committee voted 8-1 to raise the base rate from 3.75 percent to 4 percent. It is the first increase since August 2023, and it follows six straight meetings where the bank held rates steady.

Why now

The short version: inflation is climbing again, and energy prices are the reason. UK consumer price inflation hit 3.5 percent in September. The Bank of England now expects it to reach around 5 percent by the middle of next year — more than double the bank's 2 percent target.

The Bank said the conflict in the Middle East, now in its ninth month, is pushing energy prices up, and those higher energy costs are feeding into household and business bills. The bank's own words, quoted by MoneySavingExpert: "the degree of inflationary pressure in the pipeline is now sufficient to warrant an increase in Bank Rate."

The one dissenting vote is worth noting. Catherine Mann, an external member of the committee, didn't vote to hold rates — she voted for a bigger hike, to 4.25 percent. She has said the bank was slow to reassure markets it had a grip on inflation, and she worries about what she called "non-linear inflation risks" if confidence in the central bank slips.

Other members were blunt too. Deputy Governor Clare Lombardelli said there is "likely to be a case for a further increase in rates in the near term" unless inflation pressures weaken. Chief Economist Huw Pill called the quarter-point rise "a significant step" toward the stance needed to deal with threats to price stability, including the Middle East's effect on energy prices.

What a rate rise means in plain English

The base rate is what the Bank of England charges other banks when they borrow money. When it goes up, borrowing usually gets more expensive across the economy — mortgages, business loans, credit. When it goes up, savings accounts usually pay a little more too, though banks are often slower to pass on the good side than the bad side.

That's the mechanics, not advice. Nobody should make a money move off a single rate decision. But the direction matters: after years of cuts and holds, one of the world's major central banks is raising rates again, and it is telling the public more may come.

Why it matters beyond Britain

The Bank of England isn't acting alone in spirit. Reuters reports the bank has now started down a path its main peers have already embarked on — raising rates as the Middle East conflict pushes energy prices higher around the world. Oil prices have been swinging hard this week, and markets everywhere are watching the same thing: how far the energy shock spreads, and how central banks answer it.

For working people, the chain is simple and it always lands the same way. Energy costs more, so everything that runs on energy costs more, so prices rise, so the central bank raises rates to cool things down, so borrowing costs more. That squeeze is the story of this decision, and it's the story to watch in the months ahead.

The Bank of England meets again in December. If the conflict continues and inflation keeps climbing toward that 5 percent forecast, the odds are good we'll be writing about another rise.
