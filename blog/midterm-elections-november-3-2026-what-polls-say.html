Midterm Elections Are Tuesday, November 3: What the Polls Actually Say

By Wade Lee Brower Jr, October 31, 2026

Tuesday, November 3, 2026, is Election Day, and the midterms will decide who controls Congress for the next two years. All 435 seats in the U.S. House of Representatives are on the ballot, along with 35 Senate seats and 36 governorships. Here is what the polling averages actually show as voters head into the final weekend — and what they do not show.

The national picture

Every major published polling average points the same direction on the generic congressional ballot, the question that asks voters which party they would back in their House race without naming candidates. RealClearPolling's average has Democrats at 51.6 percent and Republicans at 43.3 percent, a lead of 8.3 points. NBC News's average has Democrats at 52 percent and Republicans at 43 percent. Decision Desk HQ puts it at 50.8 to 43.0, and the simple mean of five published averages, compiled by The Boston Report from data through October 27, is Democrats 51.5 percent, Republicans 43.0 percent — a margin of 8.5 points.

That national lead is why forecasters favor Democrats to take the House. The Cook Political Report's likeliest outcome is Democrats winning between 227 and 240 House seats; 218 seats make a majority. Decision Desk HQ's model projects 238 Democratic seats. But a national polling lead is not a seat count. The House is decided district by district, and a national average can move several points in either direction and still leave individual races close.

The Senate is a different fight

The Senate math is tighter. Republicans currently hold a 53-47 majority, counting the two independents who caucus with Democrats on the Democratic side. Democrats need a net gain of four seats to reach 51 and take control. The Cook Political Report rates seven Senate contests as toss-ups: Alaska, Iowa, Kansas, Maine, Michigan, Ohio, and Texas. That list alone tells you why the Senate is harder to call than the House — several of those races are in states that usually lean Republican, and several others are genuine coin flips.

What polls are, and what they are not

A poll is a snapshot of voter preference at the time it was taken, not a prediction of the result. Polls carry margins of error, and different pollsters get different answers from different samples — which is exactly why averages exist. The averages agree on direction this cycle; they differ by a point or two on size, and the range of plausible outcomes is wider than any single average suggests.

Turnout decides close races. Midterm turnout is always lower than presidential-year turnout, and which voters actually show up matters more than which voters answer a survey. If you plan to vote, check your polling place and hours with your local election office before Tuesday rather than assuming they match the last election — locations and hours change.

The bottom line is plain: Democrats enter the final days with a clear national polling lead and are favored to win the House, while the Senate remains genuinely contested. Tuesday settles it.
