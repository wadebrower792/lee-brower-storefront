Every week some new app promises to run your whole company from your
phone. I've been a carpenter and a superintendent for over thirty years,
and I build AI tools myself, so I'll give you the test I use on all of it —
including my own: **does it save time on a real job, this week, or is it a

to start without spending a dime.

## What actually works: turning your notes into words

The biggest honest win is drafting. Contractors lose their evenings to
writing — estimates, proposals, scope letters, customer emails — because
the words have to be right and typing is nobody's trade. With AI, you hand
it your rough notes and it hands you back a clean draft. You read it, fix
it, send it. The typing that ate an hour takes minutes.

But understand what the tool actually is: **the tool is the prompt, not the
chat box.** Type "write me an estimate" and you'll get generic mush that
sounds like a brochure. Give it a real instruction with your real details
and rules, and you get something you can send. Here's one you can copy
today, free, for turning a site walk into a scope draft:

> Act as an estimator for a small residential contractor. Turn my rough
> notes below into a clean scope of work for a customer estimate. Group the
> work in the order it will be done. Anything unclear, list it as a
> question for me at the end — do not invent measurements, prices, or
> materials I didn't give you.

That last line matters more than the rest. It keeps the machine honest, and
honest is the whole game.

## What actually works: the follow-up game

More bids die of silence than of price. The customer means to answer, the
week gets away from them, and the job goes to whoever stayed in front of
them. AI is good at this unglamorous work: a polite follow-up on a quote
you sent last week, a calm reply when somebody says your price is high, a
short message asking for the review while the finished job is still making
them happy. These are short pieces of writing with a formula to them —
which is exactly what prompts are for.

## What actually works: chewing through paper

The second honest win is summarising. A forty-page spec section, a long
email thread with the architect, three supplier quotes that need comparing
— paste it in and ask what it says, what it obligates you to, and what
dates are in it. Same with your own records: dictate the day's log in the
truck while it's fresh instead of reconstructing it Friday. AI is a fast
reader and a tireless filer. Let it do the reading and filing; you keep
the deciding.

## What doesn't work yet — and will cost you if you trust it

**Don't let AI price a job unsupervised.** It will produce numbers with
total confidence and zero knowledge of your costs, your market, or your
crew. Use it to draft and organise; the figures get checked against your
own price book and your own takeoff, the way they always have. **Don't
trust it to read plans on its own** — if you use any tool that counts or
measures from drawings, verify the takeoff by hand on the first jobs until
the tool has *earned* your trust, same as a new hire. And **don't expect
one magic prompt to run your office.** Vague in, vague out. Every good
result I've ever gotten came from a specific instruction, real job
details, and a human reading the output before it went anywhere. The
machine drafts. You sign. That order doesn't change.

## How to start this week

Open the free version of ChatGPT or whichever chat tool you like. Take the
scope prompt above, paste in the notes from a real walk you did recently,
and see what comes back. Then do the part that makes it yours: read every
line like a superintendent reads a sub's invoice, fix what's wrong, and
notice how long the whole thing took versus your old way. If that one
prompt earns its keep, you'll understand exactly why a whole playbook of
them exists.

AI doesn't swing a hammer, and it doesn't know your customers. What it does
is give you back the time you used to spend typing — and for a man whose
evenings are already spoken for, that's not a small thing. Start with one
prompt, on one real job, and judge it the way you'd judge anything else on
the site: by whether it holds up in the field.
