# How to Write a Construction Bid That Wins Work

By Wade Lee Brower Jr — master carpenter & construction superintendent, 30+ years

---

A bid is not a price. A price is one number on a page. A bid is the whole
document a stranger reads at their kitchen table, next to two other
contractors' documents, while they decide who they're going to trust with
their money and their house. I've spent more than thirty years on both sides
of that table — writing bids, and reading other men's bids as a
superintendent — and I'll tell you what I tell every young contractor who
asks: plenty of jobs are lost before anybody looks at the number, because
the paper didn't hold up. Here's how to write one that does.

## Start at the walk, not at the spreadsheet

The bid is won or lost during the site walk. Take notes like a man who will
have to defend every line later, because you will. Measurements, photos,
access, where the dumpster goes, what's in the way. Write down what the
customer said they want *in their words* — you'll use their words back in
the scope, and people sign papers that sound like themselves. If you price
from memory in the truck, you'll price the job you imagined, not the job
that's there.

## Write the scope in plain words, itemized

List the work in the order it will actually happen: demo, then the rough
work, then finishes. One line per piece of work, with a price or a quantity
the customer can point at. Never hand a homeowner one lump number and a
paragraph — a lump sum reads like you're hiding something, even when you're
not. An itemized bid lets the customer compare you line by line against the
other fellow, and line by line is where an honest contractor wins.

## Write down what's NOT in it

This is the section that saves your profit and your reputation. Exclusions,
spelled out: permits, haul-off, work by others, whatever you're not
carrying. And allowances — if the customer hasn't picked the tile, the
fixtures, the flooring, name a dollar allowance for it in the bid and say
plainly that the price adjusts to what they actually choose. Put this
sentence in every bid you ever send: **if it isn't listed, it isn't
included.** That one sentence has settled more arguments than any lawyer
either of us can afford.

## Know your number before you quote it

A bid built on your material cost plus a guess is a donation waiting to
happen. Your price has to carry your real labor cost — wages *plus* the
burden on top of them — a share of your overhead for the weeks the job runs,
and your profit, put in on purpose, in the open, not whatever's left over.

## Put the rules of the game in the bid

Payment schedule tied to stages of the work — deposit, progress draws,
balance on completion — never front-loaded, never "we'll figure it out."
State how long your price holds (30 days is common) because material prices
move and you're not a bank. And state the change-order rule in writing,
before the job starts: any change gets priced and signed by both sides
*before* that work begins. Mid-job is the worst possible time to negotiate
this. On paper, up front, is the only time it's cheap.

## Send it fast, then follow up

A perfect bid sitting in your truck is worth nothing. A good bid in the
customer's hands while the job is still fresh in their mind beats it every
time. And when you don't hear back, follow up — once, politely, with a
letter or message that makes it easy to say yes or ask a question. Silence
kills more bids than price does. The kit's follow-up letters exist because
